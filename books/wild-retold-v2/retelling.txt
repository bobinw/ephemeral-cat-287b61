The Gate Behind Him

Buck ruled a comfortable house in California without ever wondering who had given him the right.
He swam with the boys, walked beside the judge's daughters, and lay near the fire when the evenings cooled.
He was large, proud, and strong, and the world had so far agreed that these were reasons to treat him well.
Far away, men were searching the frozen North for gold, and strong dogs were worth money.
Manuel, a servant who needed money for gambling, took Buck out one night and sold him.
A rope tightened around Buck's throat when he resisted, and the familiar rules of his life vanished.
He traveled in a crate, thirsty and furious, with strangers laughing at the anger that shook his body.
When a man in a red sweater opened the crate, Buck sprang at him.
A club struck him down, and each new attack ended in another blow.
At last he lay still, not because his pride had gone, but because his body could not carry it any farther.
He learned that a man with a club must be obeyed, but did not learn to love that man.
It was his first lesson in a country he had not yet reached.

Snow and Teeth

In the North, Buck met snow and tried to understand the strange white substance melting on his tongue.
Nothing here behaved quite like anything at home.
Curly, a friendly dog who had traveled with him, approached another dog with a wagging tail.
The answer was a sudden bite, and when she fell, the watching dogs closed around her.
Men fought them away, but Curly was dead.
Buck remembered the sight and the laughter of Spitz, the lead dog, standing outside the struggle.
He learned never to fall when teeth were near, and began to hate Spitz.
François and Perrault harnessed the dogs to a sled, and Buck discovered that strength now had to pull a load.
The traces kept him in line, and the other dogs corrected him with sharp bites when he made mistakes.
At night he looked for a warm place and found the dogs buried in holes beneath the snow.
He dug his own hollow and curled inside, surprised that the white cold could become a blanket.
Hunger taught him to swallow food quickly, and then to steal what he needed.
The change was not an improvement in manners, but manners had little weight against an empty stomach.
Perrault was a careful traveler, and François understood dogs well enough to be firm without being needlessly cruel.
Their work required speed, but also living dogs at the end of the journey.
When Buck's feet became sore, François made little coverings for them, and the dog learned to wait for these before starting.
This was not the soft life of California, yet it showed that a hard world could still contain attention.
At the edge of camps, starving dogs sometimes rushed the team, driven by a hunger that left no room for hesitation.
Buck fought for food and a place to sleep, and every struggle added something to the fierce creature taking shape inside him.
He still remembered the man's club, but he no longer expected the men to solve every danger.
His body recovered quickly, his coat thickened, and he stopped wasting strength on movements that achieved nothing.
The old habits were disappearing, not in a single dramatic moment, but in countless small decisions made while tired and hungry.
Something that had slept through comfortable years was learning to move again.

The Place at the Front

Day after day, the runners hissed over snow while the dogs leaned into their harnesses.
Buck's muscles hardened, and senses that had been quiet in California woke inside him.
He could smell changes in the weather and hear the smallest movement at night.
He learned from the older dogs, but Spitz watched every increase in his strength.
Their quarrel spread through the team, weakening the obedience that made the sled run smoothly.
Buck did not rush into the final fight, for he had learned the cost of anger without thought.
One night a rabbit ran across the snow, and a crowd of dogs went racing after it.
The chase carried Buck and Spitz into the moonlight, where their long struggle became a fight that could have only one winner.
Spitz knew the tricks of fighting, and Buck's first attacks failed.
Then Buck changed his method, struck at the legs, and brought his enemy down.
The other dogs rushed in, and Spitz never rose again.
The next morning Buck refused any place except the front of the team.
François argued with a club, but Buck stayed beyond its reach until the man understood.
As leader, Buck demanded work and restored order, and the sled traveled faster than before.
He had won a position, but the miles still had to be pulled.
Buck's challenge to Spitz had disturbed more than two dogs.
He defended others when the leader tried to punish them, and soon dogs that had obeyed began to test the rules.
François understood the trouble and warned Buck, but a warning could not remove the struggle already growing.
When the final fight ended, the disorder had to end as well, or Buck's victory would have damaged the very position he wanted.
He made the lazy dogs pull and the stubborn ones keep their places.
He had learned the team's weaknesses while resisting its leader, and now used that knowledge to lead it himself.
The men praised the pace, and Buck took his work with the fierce seriousness he once gave to guarding the judge's house.
For a short time, the sled, the dogs, and the men seemed to understand one another.
Then orders arrived, owners changed, and the team discovered again that its wishes counted for little in human arrangements.
Buck could win the place at the front, but could not decide where the trail would end.

Too Many Miles

New men took the team onto another trail, carrying mail through the wilderness.
The work was steady, the loads heavy, and the dogs grew tired in ways that sleep could not fully mend.
Dave, one of the experienced dogs, became so ill that he could scarcely move.
He still wanted his place in the traces, and cried when another dog was put there.
To pull was the work he knew, and losing it seemed harder than the pain.
At last he could not continue, and the team went on without him after a shot sounded behind them.
Buck had heard that sound before and did not need to turn around.
The journeys continued until the worn dogs were sold to people who knew almost nothing about the North.
Hal, Charles, and Mercedes had brought too much luggage and too much confidence.
Their sled was so overloaded that the dogs could not start it, and their answer was to beat the dogs.
People watching explained the problem, but advice seemed to them like an insult.
They began the journey badly, and the wilderness had no reason to improve it for them.

The River Would Not Wait

At first the newcomers fed the dogs too much, then too little when they discovered how far they had to go.
They argued, lost time, and used the whip as though it could replace food.
The team shrank as dogs died or were left behind, and spring loosened the frozen river beneath the trail.
At John Thornton's camp, the men were warned not to go onto the ice.
Hal would not believe that nature could refuse a man determined to continue.
Buck lay down and would not rise, though the whip and club fell on him.
He knew something through his exhausted body that the people would not learn through their ears.
Thornton sprang at Hal, knocked him away, and cut Buck free.
The travelers drove the remaining dogs onto the river, angry at being delayed.
The ice broke, and the sled, the people, and the dogs disappeared into the opening.
Buck stood beside Thornton and watched the place where the trail had ended.
He had escaped, but escape had left him among the few who could remember the others.

The Man He Chose

Thornton let Buck rest, and his dogs Skeet and Nig accepted the newcomer without a fight.
Skeet cleaned Buck's wounds, and slowly the strength returned to his legs.
But what grew between him and Thornton was different from the easy comfort of his old home.
He watched the man's face, listened for his steps, and sometimes woke at night to make sure he had not vanished.
He would take Thornton's hand gently between his teeth, speaking in the only way his mouth allowed.
When Thornton was swept into a dangerous river, Buck leaped after him, though the current beat them both against the rocks.
With a rope fastened to Buck, the men on shore helped him reach Thornton and bring him back.
Later, men made a wager about whether Buck could start a heavily loaded sled and pull it one hundred yards.
Thornton had boasted of Buck's strength, but now the size of the load frightened him, and he spoke close to the dog and touched his head.
The crowd went quiet as the dog leaned into the traces.
The runners broke free of the frozen grip, and Buck pulled, step after difficult step, until the distance was covered.
The money they won opened a new journey, but no price could explain why Buck had pulled.
Before the great wager, Thornton had already seen how completely Buck would act for him.
When a man struck Thornton during a quarrel, Buck attacked the man before anyone could think to stop him.
The bond that made him gentle beside the fire could make him terrible when the man was threatened.
At the wager, the sled carried a thousand pounds, and its runners had frozen into the snow.
Simply pulling forward would not do; Buck first had to break the runners loose by working from side to side.
Men who had spoken loudly over the bet fell quiet enough to hear the harness strain.
Buck gathered himself, shifted the load, then put his whole strength into the movement.
The distance was measured, the crowd followed, and Thornton walked behind, speaking to him through the effort.
At the end, a rich man offered to buy Buck for a large sum.
Thornton refused, and returned to the dog instead of continuing the bargaining.
Buck had been sold once by a man who cared for money more than him, and now belonged beside a man who would not sell him at all.
The journey into the unknown began with that difference between them.

Something Older Than Memory

Thornton and his companions traveled farther into the wilderness and found gold in a quiet valley.
While they worked, Buck roamed through the forest, where each smell seemed to promise another life.
By the fire he sometimes dreamed of a strange, hairy man crouching in fear of the darkness.
The dream seemed older than the house in California, older even than Buck himself.
One night he followed a sound and met a thin gray wolf.
The wolf was cautious, but gradually they touched noses and ran together among the trees.
The forest called without words, and Buck understood it with his whole body.
Then he remembered Thornton and turned back, leaving the wolf behind.
He could travel farther and hunt more fiercely each time, yet the man remained the place to which he returned.
For a while, love and the wild held him between them.

The Last Tie

After a long hunt, Buck came back through woods that felt wrong before he saw the camp.
The usual sounds were missing, and the smells of strangers lay across the trail.
He found the dogs and the men dead, and followed Thornton's scent to a pool from which it did not return.
The attackers were still there, and Buck charged among them in a fury beyond anything he had known.
The survivors fled, leaving him beside a silence that could not answer his voice.
He stayed through the day, with an emptiness like hunger that no food could fill.
When the wolves came that night, he stood in the moonlight and faced their attacks until they drew back.
Then the thin gray wolf approached, and Buck recognized the brother with whom he had run.
They touched noses, and an old wolf raised his head and howled.
Buck answered, and this time no living voice at the camp called him back.
He ran with the Pack, and stories grew about a great dog moving like a ghost through the forest.
Yet in summer he returned alone to the valley where grass covered the remains of the camp.
For a time he stood beside the pool, then lifted his voice into the still air.
The wild had taken him, but it had not taken away everything he remembered.
