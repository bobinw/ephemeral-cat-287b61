The Smallest Guest

The night wind moved through the hills, carrying the smells that told Father Wolf it was time to hunt.
His cubs were asleep in the cave when Tabaqui, the jackal, arrived with news he enjoyed far too much.
Shere Khan had moved his hunting ground, and tonight the tiger was hunting a human child.
Father Wolf disliked both the news and the messenger, but before he could act, a small figure appeared among the leaves.
The child had no clothes, no claws, and not enough sense to be afraid of a wolf.
He pushed into the cave and settled among the cubs as though someone had saved a place for him.
Father Wolf lifted him gently in his mouth, without leaving a mark on his skin.
Then Shere Khan's great head filled the entrance, and his voice demanded what he called his prey.
Mother Wolf rose, and the tiger found himself looking into eyes that would not move aside.
She told him that the child would live with her cubs, and might one day hunt the hunter himself.
The narrow entrance kept the tiger's shoulders outside, and at last he backed away, furious.
Mother Wolf named the child Mowgli, the little frog, because his skin was so bare and smooth.
But her love was not enough to give him a place in the Pack, and the wolves would have to decide.

A Life Bought With a Bull

At the Council Rock, the cubs were brought before the Pack, and Akela watched from his high stone.
The old leader had survived because he thought before he leaped, a useful habit in the jungle.
Shere Khan shouted from outside the circle that the human cub belonged to him.
The Law required voices from outside the child's own family before the Pack could accept him.
Baloo, the brown bear who taught the cubs, said he would speak for the boy.
Then Bagheera slid into the moonlight, black as shadow and smooth as water.
The panther offered a freshly killed bull as the price of the child's life.
Hungry wolves listened more carefully when meat entered the argument.
They accepted Mowgli, though Bagheera knew that an agreement made over food could weaken when hunger changed.
The boy grew among the cubs, running on bare feet and sleeping with his head against warm fur.
He could pull thorns from the wolves' paws and look into their eyes until they turned away.
He thought these were ordinary things, but Bagheera watched them and remembered that Mowgli was human.
Bagheera also taught Mowgli to remember the bull that had paid for his place among the wolves.
Because of that payment, he must not kill cattle for food, even when the hunting would be easy.
The lesson put a boundary around his strength, and strength without boundaries was what made Shere Khan dangerous.
Mowgli climbed high branches and learned to move where the wolves could not follow.
At other times he lay in the sun and treated the jungle as a place arranged for his comfort.
Bagheera did not let him remain ignorant of its dangers merely because childhood felt pleasant.
The panther could be tender, but his voice was the voice of a hunter when a warning mattered.
Baloo knew that the boy learned quickly and so demanded more, not less, from him.
Mowgli found this unfair, because praise seemed much more comfortable than another lesson.
The two teachers sometimes disagreed, but neither confused loving the child with letting the jungle kill him.
Their work was to make him understand a world that would not stop being dangerous when he grew tired of learning.

Words Stronger Than Teeth

Baloo's lessons were not always pleasant, and Mowgli complained when a heavy paw corrected his mistakes.
Bagheera told the bear that kindness might teach more than bruises, but Baloo feared what ignorance could cost the boy.
The jungle had hunting grounds, warnings, and rules for creatures that did not share the same teeth.
A stranger had words to use before drinking in another hunting ground, and travelers had ways to ask for protection.
Mowgli learned the words that asked birds, snakes, and hunters to recognize him as one of their own blood.
These words were doors in places where a human hand could not open a door.
But the Monkey People had no law they would remember, no leader they would obey, and no work they would finish.
They talked of great plans, forgot them, and began again with louder voices.
Mowgli liked their praise after Baloo's difficult lessons, which made praise particularly dangerous.
The monkeys promised him importance, though they could not explain what important thing they intended to do.

The City With No People

While Mowgli slept, monkeys seized him and carried him through the high branches.
Below him the forest moved like a green river, and his friends could not follow their road through the air.
He saw Chil the kite and called the words Baloo had taught him.
Chil agreed to follow and tell the bear and panther where the monkeys went.
They reached the Cold Lairs, the ruins of a city whose builders were long gone.
The monkeys boasted about its broken halls as though they had made every stone.
They promised food, then forgot the promise in an argument over Mowgli's future greatness.
Hungry and trapped, he understood the difference between many words and a single useful action.
Baloo and Bagheera sought Kaa, the great python, because monkeys feared him more than any other hunter.
At the ruins, Bagheera fought until he had to retreat into water, while Baloo struggled under a crowd of monkeys.
Kaa arrived, broke through stone to free Mowgli, and brought the battle to an end.
Then the snake began a slow movement that held the watching monkeys in a terrible stillness.
Even Baloo and Bagheera were caught by it, but Mowgli, untouched, pulled them away.
He had been rescued by lessons he once disliked, and his teachers had been rescued by the pupil they had come to save.
Finding Kaa had not been easy, and speaking to him required more respect than shouting for help.
The python was old enough to remember things that the younger animals treated as stories.
Bagheera knew that Kaa disliked the monkeys' insults and used that knowledge while asking for his strength.
Even a rescue could involve pride, hunger, and careful bargaining.
When Chil brought news of the Cold Lairs, Bagheera went ahead quickly, while Baloo followed as fast as his heavy body allowed.
Mowgli heard the fighting outside his stone prison and called the words that kept the snakes near him from striking.
He was using one lesson to stay alive while another brought his friends across the forest.
Afterward he thanked Kaa and offered to help him with future hunting.
He did not pretend that everything had been a game, or that rescuers could be called without a cost.
Bagheera made certain that the boy faced punishment for ignoring his teachers, though the danger was over.
Then the matter was finished, and no one kept the mistake alive merely to make the child feel small.
Mowgli returned home with his friends, carrying both the memory of fear and a better understanding of the Law.

A Flower That Burned

As Akela grew old, younger wolves listened increasingly to Shere Khan.
The tiger promised them easy meat and reminded them that the boy could make them lower their eyes.
Mowgli thought the Pack was his family, and did not understand how family could become an enemy.
Bagheera showed him a mark beneath his chin, where a collar had once rubbed away the fur.
The panther had been born among humans and knew what a cage meant.
He told Mowgli to fetch the Red Flower from the village, for the animals feared fire and never called it by its true name.
Mowgli stole a pot of glowing coals and fed it until he could carry fire to the Council Rock.
Akela had missed a kill, and the wolves used that failure as their reason to remove him.
They also demanded Mowgli, while Shere Khan waited for the argument to end in his favor.
The boy scattered fire before them, and suddenly those who had called him helpless drew back.
He protected Akela and struck the tiger with a burning branch, promising to return with Shere Khan's skin.
But when the danger passed, something wet ran down his face.
He thought he was dying, until Bagheera explained that these were human tears.
He said goodbye to Mother Wolf and went toward the village, carrying a pain no fire could drive away.

The Man-Cub Among Men

A woman named Messua welcomed Mowgli and wondered if he could be the son a tiger had taken from her.
She gave him food, clothes, and a place to sleep, though sleeping inside walls felt like being caught.
He learned human words quickly, but their customs were harder.
People grew angry over things that the jungle would not notice, and believed stories that his own eyes knew were wrong.
Buldeo, the village hunter, told grand tales about the tiger while Mowgli listened with growing impatience.
It was strange to hear a man praised for knowing a creature he understood so poorly.
The boy was sent to herd buffaloes, and outside the village Gray Brother came with news.
Shere Khan was waiting to kill him and had eaten a heavy meal.
The tiger would be slow, and Mowgli knew the land well enough to use that weakness.
With Gray Brother and Akela helping, he divided the buffaloes and drove them toward opposite ends of a ravine.
The tiger was trapped below high walls as the heavy bodies thundered toward him.
Shere Khan had hunted the child for years, but could not climb away from the herd.
The buffaloes passed over him, and the hunt ended.
The plan depended on more than pointing buffaloes at an enemy.
Mowgli knew that a full meal would weigh on Shere Khan, and that the steep walls would prevent an easy escape.
He also knew that the herd could turn on him if driven badly, and that anger was no substitute for timing.
Akela separated the bulls from the cows and calves, while Gray Brother helped hold the second group.
Mowgli rode on Rama, the great bull, and led the heavy animals around the ground above the ravine.
He waited until both groups were ready, then called down to the tiger.
The voice woke Shere Khan, and the tiger looked for a way up before the herd was fully upon him.
There was no place wide enough for his escape, and the sound of hooves filled the narrow valley.
Afterward the buffaloes needed time to settle, and the boy returned them to order before turning to the dead tiger.
Removing a tiger's skin was long, difficult work for small human hands, even hands raised in the jungle.
Mowgli worked patiently while the wolves helped, for his promise was not finished merely because his enemy had fallen.
The hide would have to reach the Council Rock, where those who had rejected him could see what their chosen tiger had become.

The Price of a Tiger's Skin

Mowgli began removing the skin to keep his promise to the Pack.
Buldeo arrived, saw the dead tiger, and wanted the reward for killing it.
He ordered Mowgli aside, but Akela held the hunter down until the boy told him to leave.
Buldeo returned to the village with a story that turned his fear into proof of evil magic.
When Mowgli came home with the herd, stones flew toward him.
The villagers called him a sorcerer, and the gate that Messua had opened was closed against him.
She ran forward to protect him, and her kindness kept him from answering the village with the wolves' teeth.
He had been driven out by wolves for being human and by humans for being too much like a wolf.
Under the stars he went back to the jungle, carrying the tiger's skin but no clear answer about where he belonged.

A Company of His Own

Mother Wolf came slowly from the cave and saw that her promise to the tiger had come true.
Bagheera welcomed Mowgli, and together they spread the skin across the Council Rock.
Since Akela had lost his place, the wolves had hunted as they pleased and suffered for their carelessness.
Some had been trapped or wounded, and others never came back.
Now they wanted their old leader and the human cub to guide them again.
Bagheera reminded them that they had asked for freedom from leadership and had received it.
Mowgli would not pretend that their request had erased the night of his rejection.
He said he would hunt on his own, and his four wolf brothers came to stand beside him.
They would hunt with him, whether the Council approved or not.
He had the skin of his enemy beneath his feet and the ache of two lost homes in his heart.
Those feelings did not cancel each other, and growing older would not be as simple as winning a hunt.
For now, the paths were open, the brothers were close, and the jungle knew his voice.
