The Guest with the Sea Chest
I have been asked to write down everything I remember about Treasure Island, except where it lies.
There is treasure still buried there, and I have no wish to help anyone find it.
My name is Jim Hawkins, and our adventure began at the Admiral Benbow, the inn my father kept beside a lonely stretch of coast.
One morning a tall, brown-faced sailor arrived, dragging a heavy sea chest behind him.
A pale scar crossed his cheek, and his coat looked as though it had spent more nights at sea than on land.
He stood outside examining the bay, then asked whether many people came along our road.
When my father said very few, the sailor seemed pleased.
He paid some money, ordered rum, and told us to call him captain.
No ship followed him into the bay, and no crew came looking for orders.
He walked the cliffs by day with a telescope; at night he sat by the fire drinking and singing.
His song was about a dead man's chest, and the room grew quieter whenever he began it.
Sometimes he forced the guests to listen to stories of storms, hangings, and ships that had disappeared.
I could scarcely bear the stories, yet I scarcely wanted him to stop.
He promised me a small monthly payment if I kept watch for a sailor with one leg.
That man entered my dreams long before I ever saw him.
I imagined him springing over walls, dragging himself through hedges, and knocking at our door after dark.
Only Dr. Livesey seemed able to face the captain without lowering his eyes.
When the captain drew a knife during an argument, the doctor calmly threatened to have him punished by the law.
The knife went back into its pocket, but the captain's fear did not go away.
He listened to every new step outside as though the road itself were hunting him.

The Black Spot
The first visitor arrived on a cold morning while the captain was out.
He was pale, and two fingers were missing from one hand.
He asked about a man called Bill, and made me stand beside him while we waited.
When the captain returned, the stranger greeted him as an old shipmate.
But the captain's face turned the colour of ash.
They sent me away, and soon their low voices became shouts.
Steel rang against steel; the stranger burst out of the door with blood on his shoulder.
The captain tried to follow, then staggered and fell.
Dr. Livesey treated him and warned that more rum would kill him.
When we were alone, the captain told me that the visitor was Black Dog, and worse men would follow.
He had something they wanted, something inside the sea chest.
My father died during those unhappy days, and our own grief left little room for the captain's secrets.
Then a blind man appeared at our door, tapping the road with a stick.
He spoke gently until I took his hand; then his fingers closed like iron around my wrist.
He forced me to lead him to the captain, and pressed something into the old sailor's palm.
It was a piece of paper, black on one side, with a deadline written on the other.
The captain stared at it, rose as if to run, and suddenly fell dead.
My mother would not leave until she had recovered the money he owed us.
We opened the chest: old clothes, a watch, coins from many countries, and a wrapped packet lay inside.
While she counted only what was due, I heard the blind man's stick tapping outside again.
I took the packet, and we escaped into the darkness just before the men reached the inn.
Hidden nearby, I heard them searching, breaking things, and shouting that the papers were gone.
Mounted officers came along the road; the men scattered, and the blind leader, Pew, was struck down by a horse.
The inn was wrecked, but the packet was still safe beneath my coat.

A Map and a Sea Cook
Dr. Livesey and Squire Trelawney opened the packet that very night.
One paper recorded years of payments, with crosses where names ought to have been.
The other was a map of an island, showing hills, an anchorage, and three red crosses.
Beside one cross were directions to the treasure of Captain Flint.
I had heard that name in the captain's frightening stories.
The squire sprang up, already arranging a ship in his imagination.
The doctor agreed to go, but insisted that we must keep the map secret.
The squire promised; unfortunately, promising silence was much easier for him than keeping it.
In Bristol he bought a schooner called the Hispaniola and hired a crew.
The man who helped him find sailors was our cook, Long John Silver.
Silver kept an inn called the Spy-glass, and could move surprisingly quickly on his crutch.
He had only one leg.
I remembered the captain's warning, but this cheerful, clean, sensible man seemed quite unlike my nightmares.
Then I saw Black Dog sitting in his inn.
The man fled before we could catch him, and Silver made such a show of anger that I began to feel ashamed of my doubts.
He took me through the busy docks, explaining ships and rigging until the whole harbour seemed to open before me.
Our captain, Smollett, was much less pleased with the expedition.
Everyone seemed to know more about the treasure than he did.
He demanded that the weapons be moved to a safer place, and our own men sleep near them.
The squire thought him difficult; the doctor thought it wise to listen.
I was too excited by the sails and the water to care much about either opinion.
When we sailed, Silver stood in his galley smiling, with a parrot on his shoulder.
He called the bird Captain Flint; it could shout for gold as loudly as any sailor aboard.

Inside the Apple Barrel
During the voyage, Silver made himself useful to everyone.
He cooked, told stories, and remembered just how each man liked to be spoken to.
To me he was kind, and I would have trusted him with almost anything.
One evening I climbed into the apple barrel to find a last piece of fruit.
There was little left, and I settled down in the darkness, half asleep.
Then Silver sat down close outside, and began talking in a voice I did not know.
He had sailed with Flint, he said, and many of our crew had been pirates too.
They were waiting for the treasure to be found; afterwards they would take the ship.
A younger sailor wanted to act at once, but Silver stopped him.
Why kill useful gentlemen before they had done the useful work?
He spoke of murder as comfortably as he usually spoke of supper.
I pressed myself against the wooden side and hardly dared to breathe.
The man who had shown me the harbour was still there, yet every word had changed him.
One sailor asked for an apple, and my heart seemed to stop.
Silver suggested a drink instead; a moment later someone shouted that land was in sight.
The men hurried away, and I crawled out with knees that would scarcely hold me.
I found an opportunity to speak privately to the doctor, the squire, and Captain Smollett.
They listened without interrupting, which frightened me more than questions would have done.
There were very few of us who could certainly be trusted.
Across the deck, Silver was pointing out the island's hills and offering advice about the anchorage.
I watched his friendly face and wondered whether he could hear my thoughts.
The captain decided to wait and watch, keeping our fear as carefully hidden as the map.

The Man Among the Trees
The island rose before us with grey hills, thick woods, and a heat that seemed to lie on the water.
I had dreamed of treasure, but the sight of that coast filled me with an unexpected dislike.
The crew grew restless, so the captain allowed some of them to go ashore.
I slipped into one of the boats without asking permission.
As soon as we landed, I ran into the woods, leaving Silver's calls behind me.
I pushed through leaves and hanging branches until voices made me stop.
Silver was trying to persuade an honest sailor, Tom, to join the mutiny.
Then a terrible cry came from farther away, and Tom demanded to know what it meant.
Silver admitted that another sailor had been killed.
Tom turned his back on him and walked away.
Silver threw his crutch, knocked him down, and finished the attack with a knife.
I crouched among the leaves, sick with terror, unable to make a sound.
When I could move again, I ran without knowing where I was going.
A figure sprang behind a tree ahead of me, and I stopped so sharply that I nearly fell.
It was a man, dressed in scraps, his face darkened by the sun.
He watched my gun, then came forward, speaking like someone whose words had waited a long time for a listener.
His name was Ben Gunn, and he had been abandoned on the island for three years.
He asked whether our ship was Flint's, and trembled when I mentioned Silver.
Then he caught my sleeve and asked, quite seriously, whether we had any cheese.
There was something almost funny about it, but neither his fear nor his hunger was a joke.
He hinted that he might help our gentlemen, and told me where he had hidden a little boat.
Before I could ask more, a gun boomed across the trees.
Above a rough wooden fort, I saw our flag flying.

The Flag Above the Stockade
By the time I reached the fort, our friends had left the ship and taken shelter behind its wooden fence.
Their boat had been fired on as they crossed, and some supplies had gone into the water.
I learned these things afterwards; at that moment I was simply glad to see friendly faces.
The doctor listened closely to my account of Ben Gunn.
Captain Smollett gave me work to do, and work was a welcome change from running and hiding.
Our shelter was a log house, built inside a fence, with a spring of fresh water nearby.
Sand blew across the ground, and the air seemed full of smoke even when no guns were firing.
The next morning, Silver came carrying a white flag.
He called himself captain now, and asked for the map.
In return, he offered to spare our lives and let us leave the island.
Smollett sat before him and refused every part of the bargain.
Silver could have a fair trial if he surrendered; he would not have our treasure map.
For a moment the two captains looked at each other in silence.
Then Silver's friendly voice vanished, and he promised an attack.
He could not easily rise from where he had been sitting, and nobody stepped forward to help.
I watched him struggle to his feet, furious, and wished the fence were much higher.
The attack came sooner than I expected.
Men rushed the fence; gun smoke hid the trees, and shots struck the logs above my head.
Some climbed inside, and we fought among the sand and wooden walls.
I remember noise, a flash of a blade, and the desperate wish to be anywhere else.
At last the survivors fled, leaving us with wounded men and far fewer bullets.
The captain had been hurt badly enough that he could no longer lead another fight.
We had held the fort, but holding it had cost more than I had imagined.

A Boat No Bigger Than a Basket
That afternoon the doctor took his hat, pistols, and sword, and went alone into the woods.
I guessed he was looking for Ben Gunn, but I did not stay long enough to ask.
The log house was hot, the wounded men were groaning, and beyond the fence lay the whole island.
I filled my pockets with biscuits, took my pistols, and slipped away.
It was foolish to leave my friends so short of help; I knew it even then.
But once the trees hid the fort, I kept going.
Near a white rock I found Ben Gunn's little boat, made from a rough frame covered with skin.
It looked more like a basket than anything fit to carry a person to sea.
I waited for darkness, watching the Hispaniola at anchor and the tide moving through the narrow water.
Only two pirates had been left aboard, and their voices travelled loudly across the bay.
If I could cut the anchor rope, the tide might carry the ship away from them.
I launched the little boat, climbed in, and discovered how little it cared for my directions.
Every stroke turned it in an unexpected direction, until I let the current do most of the work.
At last the ship's great dark side rose above me.
I caught the rope, but felt its pull so strongly that I dared not cut straight through.
I worked until only a few strands remained, then waited for the rope to slacken.
From above came a crash and an angry shout; the two men were fighting in the cabin.
When the strain eased, I cut the last strands, and the schooner began to move.
For a while my little boat drifted beside her, close enough for me to hear the quarrel within.
Then the current caught us both and drew us towards the open sea.
There was no shore I could reach in the darkness, and no strength left for fighting the water.
I lay low in the boat, listening to the waves, and at last fell asleep.

The Ship and the Wounded Sailor
Morning found me off a coast where great waves broke against the rocks.
I could see land, but landing there would have smashed my little boat to pieces.
Then I saw the Hispaniola moving strangely across the water, turning as if nobody held her wheel.
The wind carried her towards me; I climbed aboard just before my own boat was crushed alongside.
One of the pirates lay dead on the deck.
The other, Israel Hands, sat wounded among scattered bottles.
He watched me quietly, and asked for something to drink.
I fetched food and drink, but kept my pistols close.
I told him that I had taken command, and lowered the pirates' flag.
Hands seemed to accept this surprisingly well.
He offered to help me sail into the North Inlet, where we could beach the ship.
I knew nothing of bringing such a vessel through a narrow channel, so I agreed.
He gave his orders, and I worked the sails and wheel, proud and frightened at the same time.
Once he asked me to go below for wine, though there was drink beside him already.
Instead of going far, I crept back and watched.
He dragged himself across the deck, found a long knife, and hid it under his clothes.
When I returned, he was sitting as before, looking too weak to move.
I held my breath and went on steering, with one eye on the channel and the other on him.
As the ship touched the bank, she lurched so sharply that we both lost our footing.
Hands came at me with the knife, and I ran towards the mast.
My pistol failed: the powder had been wet during the night.
I climbed the ropes while he followed below, then found time to reload with dry powder.
He stopped, spoke softly, and tried to persuade me to come down.
Then his knife flew through the air and pinned my shoulder against the wood.
I fired both pistols; he fell backwards into the water and did not rise.
For a moment I could do nothing but stare at the place where he had vanished.
The wound was not deep, but freeing myself from the knife took more courage than I expected.
I was alone with the ship at last, and wished there were someone aboard to tell me what to do next.

Back to the Wrong Fire
I left the ship safely in the inlet and made my way towards the stockade as darkness fell.
I pictured my friends hearing what I had done, and the captain forgiving me for leaving.
Near the fort I saw a fire burning much brighter than our own fires had burned.
That should have warned me, but I was tired and wanted to be home.
I slipped inside, feeling for a place among the sleeping men.
Something cried out for gold, and I knew the voice before the men woke.
It was Silver's parrot.
A hand caught my arm; a light appeared, and I saw pirates all around me.
Silver sat watching me, with an expression I could not read.
Our friends had given up the fort, he said, leaving it and its stores to the pirates.
They had also given him the map.
I thought he must be lying, but he showed it to me.
I could not understand why the doctor would hand over the very thing we had risked our lives to keep.
Silver wanted me to join them; the other men wanted something much worse.
With nowhere left to hide, I told them that I had heard their plan in the apple barrel.
I had warned our friends, cut the ship loose, and taken her where they would not find her easily.
If they killed me, I said, they would gain nothing by it.
The words sounded bolder than I felt.
One man rose with his knife, but Silver ordered him back.
He planted his crutch before him and reminded them who was captain.
The men went outside to discuss replacing him, leaving us together.
Then Silver's voice changed again.
He knew the game might be lost, and wanted me to help him escape punishment if he kept me alive.
I agreed to speak for him if I could; at least we now understood what each of us wanted.

A Bargain in the Morning
The men came back carrying another black spot, this one cut from a Bible.
Silver scolded them for damaging the book before allowing them to explain their charges.
They blamed him for the failed attack, the loss of the ship, and keeping me alive.
He answered them one by one, turning their anger towards the men who had urged him to act too soon.
Then he spread the treasure map before them.
At the sight of it, their eyes shone, and the argument changed completely.
Within minutes, the black spot lay forgotten, and Silver was captain again.
He had saved himself for the moment, but I saw how carefully he watched every face.
In the morning Dr. Livesey came to treat the sick pirates.
It was strange to see him move among men who might have killed him, asking about fevers as if they were patients at home.
Silver allowed me to speak with him beside the fence, and I told him how I had taken the ship.
The doctor's eyes widened; for once I had news he had not expected.
He urged me to climb over the fence and run with him.
I wanted to, more than I could say, but I had promised Silver not to escape.
I stayed on my side of the fence, although every part of me seemed to be pulling towards the doctor.
He was angry, frightened for me, and finally quiet.
Before leaving, he warned Silver to look out for trouble when they found the treasure.
Silver asked what he meant, but the doctor would not explain.
Soon we were walking into the woods with the map, tools, and guns.
Silver held one end of a rope, and the other end was fastened to me.
He spoke kindly as we walked, but did not loosen the knot.

The Voice in the Woods
The men searched the map eagerly, arguing about trees and the shape of the hills.
Every step seemed to bring them closer to gold, and farther from remembering anything else.
Then a shout brought us to a skeleton lying beneath a tree.
Its arms stretched in one direction, as though the dead man were still pointing the way.
Silver understood: Flint had used one of his murdered men as a compass.
The pirates became quiet, and I wished we could leave the bones undisturbed.
They remembered that Flint had taken six men ashore to bury the treasure, and returned alone.
Now even the warm sunlight seemed unable to reach us properly through the branches.
Suddenly, a thin voice began singing among the trees.
It was the old song about the dead man's chest.
The men stared at one another, their faces drained of colour.
They thought they heard Flint himself, returned from the dead.
Then the voice called for rum, repeating words they remembered from his deathbed.
One pirate began praying; another wanted to run.
Silver alone kept thinking, though I could see that the sound had shaken him too.
He argued that a ghost would not make an echo, then listened again more closely.
The voice reminded him of Ben Gunn.
The men had known Ben, and were much less frightened of a living castaway than of a dead captain.
They took up their tools and began moving again.
As we approached the place marked on the map, Silver stopped speaking kindly to me.
His eyes were fixed ahead, and he pulled the rope so hard that I stumbled.
Then the first men stopped, and a cry of disbelief ran through them.
There was a great hole in the ground, with broken boards and marks of old digging.
The treasure had already been taken.

What We Carried Home
Silver changed at once: he put a pistol into my hand and moved me away from the hole.
The men looked from the empty earth to him, and I saw their disappointment turn to murder.
Before they could rush us, shots cracked from the trees.
The doctor, Gray, and Ben Gunn appeared, and the surviving pirates fled.
Silver stood beside me, pretending that this was just the ending he had intended.
On the way to Ben's cave, I finally learned why the doctor had given away the map.
Ben had discovered the treasure long before we arrived, and had spent many weary journeys carrying it to his cave.
The map led only to an empty hole.
When our ship vanished, the doctor had bargained away the useless map and the fort, and moved our friends to Ben's safer shelter.
Our friends had been watching the treasure hunters, waiting for a chance to save me.
Inside the cave, gold lay in heaps, stamped with the faces of kings from countries I had never seen.
It was heavier, dirtier, and less magical than the gold I had imagined over the map.
We spent days carrying it to the ship, while Silver worked as hard and spoke as pleasantly as anyone.
The captain would not trust him, and neither would I, though I remembered that he had kept me alive.
We left the remaining pirates on the island with supplies, tools, and ammunition.
The doctor feared their fate, but none of us wished to sail home beside men who had tried to kill us.
At a port on our way back, Silver escaped with a bag of gold; Ben had helped him get ashore.
I never saw him again, and cannot say whether he lived comfortably or ended in trouble.
We reached home with our treasure, and each man received his share.
Ben spent his money astonishingly quickly, then found work in the country, far from lonely islands.
There were still treasures left on the island, but no one could pay me enough to return for them.
Sometimes I wake at night hearing the surf, and see the woods again as they looked before a shot was fired.
Then, from somewhere in the darkness, the parrot's sharp voice calls for gold.
And I am back aboard the Hispaniola, holding my breath and listening.
