A House Without a Road

In Kansas, even the grass seemed tired of being gray.
The sun had worn the color from the little house, and the wind had worn the smiles from Aunt Em's face.
Dorothy still laughed, mostly because Toto could find a game in almost anything.
One afternoon the sky darkened, and Uncle Henry saw a storm turning across the plain.
Aunt Em hurried down into the storm cellar, but Toto hid under the bed.
By the time Dorothy caught him, the floor rose beneath her feet.
The house was inside the turning wind, sailing without a road, a horse, or any person who knew where it was going.
Dorothy held Toto tightly until the movement grew gentle, and at last she fell asleep.
When the house landed, she opened the door on grass so green that Kansas seemed like an old picture without paint.
Small people in blue clothes stood outside with a little woman in white.
They thanked Dorothy for killing the Wicked Witch of the East, whose feet stuck out from beneath the house.
Dorothy stared at the feet and wished people would not thank her for things she had never meant to do.
The good witch gave her the dead witch's silver shoes and kissed her forehead for protection.
To reach home, she said, Dorothy must ask the great Wizard of Oz in the Emerald City.
A road of yellow bricks led there, which was comforting after a journey made entirely of wind.
The little people were Munchkins, and the good witch came from the North.
Dorothy had never heard of either, but they spoke as confidently of their world as Aunt Em spoke of Kansas.
She asked whether all witches were wicked and learned that this was no more true than saying all people were alike.
The North and South had good witches, while the East and West had brought their people trouble.
The Munchkins treated Dorothy as someone important, which made her feel awkward rather than grand.
She had been asleep while the house did its terrible work, and could take no pride in her victory.
Before setting out she found food in the house and put on a clean dress, for a long road was no reason to forget ordinary preparations.
Toto ran ahead, finding that a strange country offered excellent smells.
The road led past blue houses and gardens, and people greeted the child whose accidental arrival had changed their lives.
That evening a friendly Munchkin gave her a place to sleep and spoke of dangers farther along the road.
Dorothy listened, but could not decide to remain merely because traveling would be difficult.
Somewhere beyond this new world were two people who did not know where she was.
She hoped Aunt Em had not begun to believe that the wind had taken her forever.

The Man Who Wanted Brains

Beside the yellow road stood a Scarecrow, fixed to a pole with his painted eyes looking over a field.
Dorothy was startled when he winked, but she was a polite child and helped him down.
His legs bent whenever he asked them to be firm, and he fell often before learning to walk.
He did not mind falling, since straw was an excellent material for a person likely to meet the ground.
What troubled him was his head, which held straw instead of brains.
“If Oz can send you home, perhaps he can give me something to think with,” he said.
They went on together, and Dorothy told him about Kansas.
The Scarecrow could not understand why she wanted a gray country when this one was so beautiful.
Dorothy explained that Aunt Em was there, and that a beautiful place was not necessarily one's home.
The Scarecrow considered this carefully for someone quite certain he could not think.
At night Dorothy slept, while he watched beside her, needing neither a bed nor a dream.

An Axe, Some Oil, and a Roar

In a wood they heard a low groan and found a man made of tin standing with his axe raised.
Rain had rusted his joints, and he had been waiting so long that even calling for help was difficult.
Dorothy found his oil can and worked patiently until he could lower his arms.
He thanked her with the great relief of someone who had finally finished a movement begun long ago.
He had once been an ordinary woodman, but an enchanted axe had cost him his body piece by piece.
A tinsmith had replaced the pieces, yet had not given the new body a heart.
The Tin Woodman wanted one more than anything, so he joined the journey.
Soon a great Lion bounded onto the road and frightened Toto.
Dorothy slapped the Lion's nose for threatening a dog so much smaller than himself.
Instead of eating her, he began to complain about how unhappy he was.
Everyone expected a lion to be brave, while he expected every danger to be the end of him.
He hoped Oz might provide courage, and the little party became four travelers and a dog.

The Things They Did Not Have

The road grew rough, and a deep gap cut across their way.
The Scarecrow suggested that the Lion jump across carrying them one at a time.
The Lion was afraid of falling, but he made the jumps all the same.
When a wider gap appeared, the Tin Woodman cut down a tree to make a bridge.
Creatures with bodies like bears and heads like tigers came after them, and the Lion turned to face them.
The Scarecrow thought of cutting the bridge before the creatures could cross.
The Tin Woodman's axe did the work, and their enemies fell into the gap.
Later the travelers entered a field of poppies whose sweet smell made Dorothy and the Lion sleep.
The tin man and the straw man could carry Dorothy out, but the Lion was too heavy.
The Tin Woodman saved a little field mouse from a wildcat, simply because he could not bear to see it killed.
She was the queen of the mice, and her people came in thousands to pull the Lion out on a cart.
Thus the man without brains made plans, the beast without courage faced danger, and the man without a heart made friends by kindness.
But each still believed that Oz had something important to give.
Before the poppy field, they had to cross a river on a raft made by the Tin Woodman.
The current carried them away from their intended landing, and the Scarecrow's pole stuck in the muddy bottom.
He clung to it while the raft moved on, leaving him high above the water with no useful way to walk home.
When the others reached shore, a stork heard the difficulty and flew to fetch him.
Being made of straw was not much help in feeling clever, but it was wonderfully helpful in being carried by a bird.
The friends thanked the stork and went on together, unwilling to count any one of them as an acceptable loss.
After the field mice saved the Lion, Dorothy saw how an act done without expecting payment could return in an unexpected shape.
The Tin Woodman worried constantly about stepping on small creatures, and once his tears rusted his jaw.
Dorothy oiled him, though she wondered how someone so gentle could be certain that he had no heart.
The Scarecrow preferred brains, because a person without them could not know what to do with a heart.
The Tin Woodman preferred a heart, because cleverness seemed a poor comfort without happiness.
Neither convinced the other, and their disagreement made the long road more interesting without making their friendship weaker.

Four Visits to One Wizard

At the Emerald City, a guardian locked green glasses over their eyes before letting them pass the gates.
Everything shone green, and the city looked wealthy enough to solve almost any difficulty.
Oz received the travelers separately, which was not how Dorothy would have welcomed guests.
To her he appeared as an enormous head; to the Scarecrow, as a beautiful lady.
The Tin Woodman saw a terrible beast, and the Lion saw a ball of fire.
Each appearance gave the same answer: first they must destroy the Wicked Witch of the West.
Dorothy had come to ask for a way home, not for employment as a destroyer of witches.
Still, the others needed their gifts, and no easier road seemed open.
They left the green city and traveled west, watched by a witch whose single eye could see far away.

A Bucket of Water

The witch sent wolves, crows, and black bees against the travelers, but their different bodies and skills saved them.
At last she used a Golden Cap that could command the Winged Monkeys three times.
The monkeys tore the Scarecrow apart and dropped the Tin Woodman onto sharp rocks.
They carried the Lion away, but would not hurt Dorothy because of the good witch's kiss.
At the castle Dorothy had to work, while the Lion was kept prisoner.
The witch wanted the silver shoes but feared the power that Dorothy did not know how to use.
She set an invisible bar across the floor, and Dorothy tripped, losing one shoe.
The witch snatched it, and Dorothy, angry at this miserable trick, threw a bucket of water over her.
The great enemy began to melt, complaining as she sank toward the floor.
Dorothy watched in astonishment, for she had meant to punish the witch, not wash her entirely away.
The castle's workers were free, and they helped put the Scarecrow together and mend the Tin Woodman.
Dorothy found the Golden Cap and used it to send the reunited company back to the Emerald City.

The Small Man Behind the Great Name

Oz delayed seeing them, which seemed odd for a wizard who had asked them to do such important work.
When they finally entered, the Lion roared, Toto jumped aside, and a screen fell over.
Behind it stood a little old man who was not a head, a lady, a monster, or a fire.
The wonderful forms had been his devices, operated from behind the screen.
He had arrived by balloon and found that people were willing to call him a great wizard.
“Then you are not wonderful at all?” Dorothy asked.
He admitted that he was an ordinary man who had become very good at keeping a secret.
Nevertheless he gave the Scarecrow a head filled with bran, pins, and needles.
He gave the Tin Woodman a silk heart and the Lion a drink he called courage.
The gifts made them happy, though the road had already shown what they could do.
For Dorothy he prepared a balloon, but Toto ran after a cat just as it was ready.
Dorothy followed her dog, the rope broke, and Oz sailed away without her.
The truth about Oz did not make the friends cruel to him, though it left them with difficult questions.
He had frightened them with his great forms and sent them against a real witch, while he himself had no magic to protect anyone.
Still, he knew something about people and the strange weight a belief could carry.
The Scarecrow felt wiser as soon as his new head was filled, and the Lion became confident after his drink.
The Tin Woodman listened to his silk heart and was pleased, though Dorothy had never needed its sound to recognize his kindness.
Oz offered Dorothy something more practical, because a feeling of home could not carry her across the desert.
They stitched silk into a balloon, and the citizens gathered to watch the great wizard leave.
Oz told them that the Scarecrow would govern while he was away, and no one seemed troubled by putting a man of straw in charge.
The people trusted the appointment because Oz had made it, which was another kind of magic he had practiced well.
Then the balloon rose, Dorothy was left with Toto, and the city could provide almost everything except the one thing she wanted.
Her friends could have remained to enjoy their new happiness, but chose to help her finish her own journey.

Three Steps Home

The friends set out again, this time toward Glinda in the South.
Even after all their adventures, the world still had fighting trees, strange people made of china, and roads that required thought.
In a forest, the Lion killed a huge spider that frightened the other animals.
They asked him to rule them, and he promised to return once Dorothy was safe.
Glinda listened to the story, then asked about the silver shoes.
They could carry Dorothy home in three steps if she struck their heels together and told them where to go.
She could have gone at the beginning, but then the Scarecrow would still be on his pole.
The Tin Woodman would still be rusting, and the Lion would still be hiding from his own roar.
Their wishes had grown into new lives: the Scarecrow would rule the city, and the tin man would lead the people of the West.
Dorothy embraced them, finding that saying goodbye was harder than asking the shoes to work.
Then she held Toto and asked to go to Aunt Em.
The wind rushed past, the shoes were lost over the desert, and she landed on the Kansas grass.
The country was still plain and gray, but Aunt Em was running toward her.
That was quite enough to make it the best place in the world.
