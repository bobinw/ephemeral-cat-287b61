The Moving Reef
In 1866, a strange object began to trouble ships in the open sea.
It was long, fast, and sometimes surrounded by a bright light.
Captains who had never met reported the same extraordinary sight.
One called it a floating island; another called it an enormous whale.
Then a ship struck it, and a neat hole appeared in the ship's metal hull.
A floating island could not have traveled so quickly, and an ordinary whale could not have made that hole.
Newspapers argued, scientists wrote letters, and sailors began watching the water with new anxiety.
My name is Pierre Aronnax, and I study the animals of the sea.
I was in New York when the American government prepared an expedition to find the creature.
An invitation arrived for me to sail aboard the Abraham Lincoln.
I had planned to return to France, but how could I refuse such a chance?
Conseil, my faithful assistant, packed our clothes without a word of complaint.
He had a wonderful memory for the names and families of animals.
Whether he could recognize the living animal was sometimes a different question.
On the warship I met Ned Land, a Canadian whose harpoon rarely missed its mark.
He could spot a whale long before I could see anything but water.
“A whale that breaks iron?” he said, when I explained my theory.
“The deep sea is not fully known,” I replied.
“Then I shall believe in your animal when I can put a harpoon into it.”
For months we searched, crossing vast stretches of the Pacific.
Men climbed the masts until their eyes hurt, and every distant shape became a possible monster.
At last, the captain announced that we would turn back if nothing appeared within three days.
On the second night, Ned's shout rang across the deck.
Something shone below the dark water, a little distance from our ship.
Its light moved steadily, as if a great lamp were traveling beneath the waves.
We increased our speed, but the shining object increased its speed too.
The chase lasted through the night and into the following day.
Our guns fired, but the creature seemed untouched.
When we finally drew close, Ned hurled his harpoon with all his strength.
I heard it strike with a sharp metallic sound.
Metal? Before I could understand, a tremendous blow shook the Abraham Lincoln.
The rail vanished beneath my hands, and I fell into the sea.

An Iron Whale
Cold water closed over my head, and my heavy clothes dragged me downward.
I struggled to the surface and called for help.
The lights of the warship were already moving away.
Then a hand caught me, and a familiar voice spoke beside my ear.
“If master will lean on my shoulder, we can swim a little farther.”
It was Conseil, who had jumped after me without stopping to think.
We took turns resting and swimming, while the stars moved slowly overhead.
My strength was almost gone when we heard someone calling from the darkness.
Ned Land was standing on the back of our mysterious whale.
He pulled us up onto a hard, slippery surface.
I struck it with my hand and heard the same ringing sound as before.
There could be no more doubt: our whale was made of iron.
As daylight approached, the vessel began to sink.
Ned stamped on the surface and shouted until a hatch opened.
Several men appeared, seized us, and hurried us down a ladder.
We were shut in a room with a table, chairs, and no window.
Two men visited us, but they seemed not to understand our French, English, or German.
Ned tried shouting louder, which did not improve their understanding.
We received food and dry clothes, then waited for an answer.
At last, one of the strangers returned and addressed me in perfect French.
He had understood us all along.
“I am Captain Nemo,” he said, “and you are aboard the Nautilus.”
I studied his proud face and the quiet force in his voice.
He knew my name and had read my books.
But he refused to tell us his country, his past, or the purpose of his voyage.
We had discovered his secret, he explained, and could never return to the world above.
“Never?” Ned demanded.
Nemo did not soften his answer.
I should have thought only of escape; instead, I was also burning to see his extraordinary ship.
Nemo noticed my curiosity and opened the door.
“Come, Professor. You wished to know the ocean.”

All by Electricity
The room beyond the door was a library, and I stopped in astonishment.
Thousands of books stood in tall shelves, far more than I had expected to find beneath the sea.
Nemo led me next into a beautiful sitting room filled with paintings and rare shells.
Some specimens were so fine that I forgot to ask where they had come from.
“You are looking at treasures gathered by the Nautilus,” the captain said.
I asked about the lights, which burned steadily without smoke.
“Electricity,” he replied.
It lit the rooms, turned the propeller, and powered the vessel's instruments.
Nemo described his machines with the pride of an inventor showing his greatest work.
I followed him through passages toward the engine room.
The Nautilus had two hulls, one within the other, to resist the pressure of deep water.
Sea water entered special tanks to make the ship heavier and carry it downward.
When pumps emptied those tanks, the vessel could rise again.
The explanation sounded simple, but the skill of its construction amazed me.
On the wall, a needle showed our depth; another instrument showed our direction.
I began to understand how Nemo could travel where ordinary ships could not follow.
At dinner, I believed we were eating familiar food until the captain explained each dish.
Much of it came from fish and other creatures of the sea.
Even our clothes used materials that Nemo had found in the ocean.
“The sea supplies my needs,” he said.
Later, shutters drew back from two great windows in the sitting room.
Outside, our lamps spread light through the dark water.
Fish appeared as if a curtain had risen on a living theatre.
I recognized some species, but others were strangers even to me.
Conseil calmly divided them into families while Ned asked which were good to eat.
Our lamps revealed bright scales, strange fins, and mouths that opened and closed in silence.
The fish swam away, but new shapes immediately took their places.
I could have remained at that window all night.
For the first time, the sea was not a surface hiding its secrets from me.
I was traveling inside it.

A Walk in the Submarine Forest
One morning, a written invitation arrived in my cabin.
Captain Nemo invited me to hunt in the forests near the island of Crespo.
Ned's face brightened at once.
Forests meant dry land, fresh food, and perhaps a chance to leave.
The captain quickly corrected our mistake: his forest grew beneath the water.
Ned lost his enthusiasm, but mine only increased.
Nemo showed us diving suits, metal helmets, and air tanks carried on our backs.
The tanks supplied air without a long hose running to the surface.
Our lamps used electricity too, so there was no flame for the water to put out.
I examined every part with eager attention.
Then Conseil and I followed Nemo into a chamber beside the sea.
Water filled the chamber, an outer door opened, and we stepped onto the bottom.
The heavy boots that troubled me inside the ship became easier to move in the water.
Sunlight fell through the waves and trembled across the pale sand.
Every footprint sparkled, as if the ground were scattered with tiny pieces of glass.
Shells, sea stars, and delicate plants lay all around us.
Fish passed above our heads, and shadows slid over the sand.
I tried to speak, forgetting that my voice could not travel from my helmet to Conseil's.
He seemed to understand my excitement without hearing a word.
Soon we entered the forest itself.
Great sea plants rose around us, their stems reaching toward the light.
Their branches stood upright in the water rather than hanging as branches did on land.
Red, brown, and green forms filled the spaces between them.
I walked carefully, stopping again and again to examine something wonderful.
Nemo moved as easily as a man strolling through his own garden.
After many hours, we rested on the bottom and then turned toward the Nautilus.
Near the end of our walk, two enormous sharks passed close by.
Nemo signaled us to lie flat, and I pressed myself against the sand.
The shadows moved slowly over us and disappeared.
Only then did I understand why the captain had carried a weapon into his beautiful forest.

An Island and a Coral Grave
Our journey carried us west across the Pacific, through waters rich in islands and dangerous reefs.
Near New Guinea, the Nautilus struck a reef and stopped.
I expected the captain to be alarmed, but he consulted his tide tables.
In a few days, he said, a higher tide would lift the ship free.
Until then, he allowed us to visit a nearby island in the boat.
Ned needed no second invitation.
He stepped ashore with the delight of a man returning home after years away.
There were fruit trees, birds, and a warmth in the air that no electric lamp could provide.
While I examined unfamiliar plants, Ned searched for something to put in a cooking pot.
“Professor, you may classify dinner after we have eaten it,” he told me.
Conseil helped him prepare the meal and helped me collect specimens.
For a short time, we could almost forget the submarine waiting offshore.
But islanders appeared, and we withdrew to the boat as the gathering grew larger.
Some followed us toward the Nautilus, and I feared they might enter through the open hatch.
Nemo remained calm: electricity ran through the outer railing.
Anyone who touched it quickly drew back.
When the predicted tide arrived, the Nautilus rose from the reef exactly as Nemo had promised.
Once again, the captain's knowledge had opened a path where I had seen none.
Later in the voyage, something happened that Nemo would not explain.
We were ordered to remain in our cabin, and after eating, we fell into an unusually heavy sleep.
When I awoke, the captain asked whether I knew medicine.
He took me to a sailor whose head was terribly injured.
I examined the man and could offer no hope.
Nemo's hand tightened, and tears appeared in eyes I had thought too proud to weep.
The next day, he led us on another walk beneath the sea.
This time, his men carried a body.
We entered a quiet place where coral branches formed a kind of living chapel.
A grave was opened, and the crew lowered their companion into it.
They knelt around him without a sound that could reach me through the water.
On returning, Nemo told me that his dead rested there, beyond the reach of men.
“Or sharks?” I asked.
His answer came slowly: men were the greater danger he wished to keep away.

The Pearl and the Shark
In the Indian Ocean, Captain Nemo offered to show us the pearl banks near Ceylon.
I accepted with pleasure until he mentioned that sharks also visited those waters.
Suddenly, I found reasons to ask several more questions about the plan.
Ned, who was less troubled by sharks than I was, sharpened his harpoon.
We left the Nautilus before dawn and reached shallow water in the boat.
After putting on our suits, we followed Nemo between rocks and beds of oysters.
These shells looked unremarkable from outside, yet some could hide a pearl.
A tiny object lodged inside a shell could slowly become covered in shining layers.
The result was a treasure for which people risked their lives.
Nemo led me into a small underwater cave and opened an enormous shell.
Inside lay a pearl unlike any I had seen.
I stared at it, imagining what it might be worth.
But the captain closed the shell carefully and left the pearl to continue growing.
Outside the cave, we noticed a diver working alone.
He had no metal helmet and no tank of air.
Holding his breath, he sank to the bottom, gathered shells, and hurried back to the surface.
He repeated the effort while we watched from the shelter of a rock.
Then I saw a great shape moving toward him.
A shark!
The diver saw it too late.
Nemo rushed forward with his knife, drawing the shark away from the helpless man.
The creature turned on the captain, and a fierce struggle began.
I could not move; it was as if fear had made my heavy boots part of the rock.
Nemo was knocked down, with the shark above him.
At that instant, Ned drove his harpoon into the creature.
The water darkened, and the shark rolled away.
We brought the diver to the surface and helped him breathe again.
He looked from our strange suits to his little boat, hardly understanding what had happened.
Before leaving, Nemo put a bag of pearls into his hands.
Later, the captain spoke to me of the man's poverty and the suffering of his people.
I had seen a different Nemo that morning, yet I still could not place all his actions together.
Ned had saved him, and the captain thanked the Canadian with a firmness neither man would forget.

The Arabian Tunnel
When the Nautilus entered the Red Sea, I studied our route on the chart.
The land at Suez lay between us and the Mediterranean.
I assumed we must turn south and sail all the way around Africa.
Nemo heard my calculation and gave me a curious look.
“Why go around the land, Professor, when we can go beneath it?”
I asked him to explain, certain I had misunderstood.
He had discovered a natural passage through the rock below Suez.
He called it the Arabian Tunnel.
“Did you find it by chance?” I asked.
“Chance helped,” he said, “but reasoning helped more.”
The same kinds of fish lived in both seas, which had given him an idea.
He caught fish near Suez, marked their tails with small copper rings, and released them.
Months later, he found some of those marked fish in the Mediterranean.
The fish had shown him that a connection existed.
The Nautilus had then found the passage itself.
I was delighted by this experiment, which began with a creature and ended with a road through the earth.
At night, Nemo invited me into the small room where the ship was steered.
Ahead of us, the lamp showed a dark opening in the rock.
We entered, and the current caught the vessel.
Stone walls rushed past on either side.
I watched the instruments, remembering the millions of tons of earth above my head.
Nemo stood at the controls, steady as if he were crossing an empty bay.
In less than twenty minutes, we were through.
The Mediterranean opened before us.
When I told Ned, he refused to believe we had traveled so far overnight.
Conseil brought the chart, and at last the Canadian had to accept the evidence.
Now there were familiar coasts nearby, and Ned began planning our escape more seriously.
I knew he was right to want land, but every day beneath the sea offered another discovery.
Farther west, Nemo showed me gold recovered from ships sunk long ago in Vigo Bay.
When I asked about its purpose, he spoke of people in need and nations suffering under powerful rulers.
He had left the land, but he had not forgotten everyone who lived upon it.

The Name on the Rock
One night in the Atlantic, Nemo offered me a walk unlike any we had taken before.
We left the Nautilus in our diving suits and climbed a steep underwater slope.
There was a red glow in the distance, too deep to come from the moon.
Broken trees lay on the ground, their trunks turned hard as stone.
As we climbed, the red light grew stronger.
I began to see walls and columns among the strange shapes below us.
These were not natural rocks.
They had once formed buildings, and streets ran between them.
An underwater volcano glowed nearby, casting light over an immense ruined city.
I stopped, trying to take in the impossible sight.
Where were we?
What people had built these walls, and what disaster had brought the ocean over them?
I turned toward Nemo, forgetting again that I could not speak through the water.
He picked up a pale piece of stone and wrote a single name upon a dark rock.
ATLANTIS.
The lost land of old stories lay before me in the world Nemo had opened.
I traced the lines of distant streets with my eyes, imagining voices where only fish moved now.
Nemo remained motionless beside me.
Was he thinking of the vanished people, or of some vanished world of his own?
He gave me no answer, and we returned quietly to the ship.
Our next stop revealed another of his secrets.
The Nautilus entered a submerged opening and came to rest inside an old volcanic mountain.
Above the underground lake was a space filled with air.
The crew went ashore inside the mountain to collect coal.
“Coal?” I asked, remembering the captain's electric machines.
Nemo explained that it helped him prepare materials for his electrical power.
His independence from land required work hidden beneath it.
We explored this strange sheltered shore before the Nautilus sailed again.
Soon we crossed the Sargasso Sea, where floating plants spread over the water like a vast meadow.
To a surface ship it could seem an obstacle; below it, our road remained clear.
Every chart I owned seemed smaller after these journeys.

The Country of Ice
The farther south we traveled, the colder and stranger the sea became.
At first, separate mountains of ice drifted past our windows.
Some were blue, some green, and some white enough to dazzle the eyes.
Then they joined into a barrier stretching across the horizon.
Surely, I thought, even the Nautilus must turn back here.
Nemo announced the opposite: he intended to reach the South Pole.
“Through all that ice?” I asked.
“Under it.”
An ordinary ship needed an open surface, but our vessel could seek a passage in the water below.
The captain's idea was so bold that my objections turned into eager questions.
We filled our stores of air, closed the hatch, and went down.
Ice lay above us like the roof of an immense tunnel.
Nemo watched the depth and the shape of the ice while his men guided the ship.
At last, we reached open water on the other side of the barrier.
There were birds, seals, and a rocky shore under a cold southern sky.
We landed, and I hurried to inspect the animals and the unfamiliar ground.
Nemo's attention was fixed on the sun, which remained low above the horizon.
He needed observations to establish how far south we had traveled.
Clouds interfered, and we waited impatiently for a clear sky.
When the sun became visible, Nemo took his measurements.
According to his calculations, we had reached the Pole.
He raised a black flag bearing the letter N.
The captain claimed this strange country in his own name.
I watched the flag move in the wind and wondered again who stood beside me.
But our triumph was brief.
On the journey back, an enormous iceberg overturned.
The water shook, the Nautilus was thrown sideways, and our path closed.
We tried the other direction and found ice there too.
There was ice above us, ice around us, and a thick bed of ice below.
The captain examined the instruments, then explained the danger with terrible calm.
The sea was freezing around the vessel, and our supply of breathable air was limited.
Nemo planned to cut through the ice beneath us and escape into the water below.
We put on our diving suits and joined the work.
Each team used the air tanks for a fixed time, then handed them to the next team.
The fresh air inside my helmet made the hard work almost welcome.
Inside the ship, each breath was becoming harder.
My head ached, and Conseil's usually steady voice grew weak.
Nemo sent hot water against the freezing walls to slow their growth.
Still, the excavation seemed too slow.
At last, he decided to use the weight of the Nautilus to break the thin layer that remained.
The tanks filled, the ship settled, and we listened.
A crack sounded beneath us, like paper tearing.
Then the Nautilus dropped into open water.
The pumps worked, the propeller turned, and the vessel raced north.
But we were still beneath the ice, and I no longer had enough air to remain fully awake.
Suddenly, I felt a little fresh air enter my lungs.
Ned and Conseil had saved the last air in a diving tank for me.
I tried to refuse it, but they held my hands.
The Nautilus struck the ice above us, drew back, and struck again.
When the hatch finally opened, living air rushed through the ship.
We lay on the deck and breathed, unable to do anything else.

The Eight-Armed Giant
After the ice, the open sea seemed almost gentle.
We rounded Cape Horn and traveled north along the coast of South America.
Through the windows I saw new fish, forests of sea plants, and changing forms of rock.
I filled page after page, trying to keep up with the voyage.
Ned's thoughts were elsewhere.
He spoke often of Canada, the shore, and a meal eaten beneath the sky.
The long confinement was weighing on him more heavily each day.
Even Conseil urged me to consider our return.
Then an extraordinary creature appeared outside the sitting-room window.
It had enormous eyes, a long body, and eight powerful arms.
Round suckers covered the arms, enabling them to hold whatever they touched.
Conseil studied it with great interest, while Ned looked at it with less affection.
Other giants followed, filling the water with slowly moving arms.
Then the Nautilus stopped.
One creature had caught itself in our machinery and prevented the propeller from turning.
Nemo ordered the ship to the surface, where his men prepared axes.
These were not enemies that a distant shot could remove.
We would have to fight them beside the hatch.
As the door opened, a great arm slid inside like a living rope.
Nemo struck it, and the crew rushed onto the platform.
I followed into a nightmare of waves, twisting arms, and shouts.
A sailor was seized and lifted above the deck.
He cried for help in French, and the sound pierced through the noise.
Nemo threw himself toward the arm that held him.
But a dark cloud of ink spread through the water, hiding the creature.
When we could see again, the sailor was gone.
Ned nearly fell beneath another giant, but Nemo reached him in time.
At last, the creatures drew away, leaving the platform battered and slippery.
The captain stood over the place where his sailor had disappeared.
He did not hide his tears.
For days afterward, he seemed scarcely to notice the course of his ship.
The Nautilus moved slowly, as if the vessel itself were sharing his sorrow.

A Ship on the Horizon
The Nautilus carried us into the North Atlantic, where Nemo showed me another sunken ship.
He spoke of its history with an emotion that I could not explain.
Soon afterward, we rose to the surface and sighted a warship in the distance.
Smoke climbed from its funnel, and it turned toward us.
Ned was immediately on the platform, ready to signal.
At last, here was a vessel that might take us home.
Before we could call, a shot struck the water near the Nautilus.
The warship was attacking us.
I looked at Nemo and saw an expression more frightening than the guns.
He knew this enemy, though he would not name its country.
I begged him to escape beneath the water.
Instead, he spoke of his destroyed home and of the family he had lost.
All that he had loved had been taken from him, he said.
The people behind that warship were his enemies.
I tried to speak again, but he would hear nothing more.
The captain who had saved a pearl diver now intended to destroy a ship full of men.
I returned to Ned and Conseil, horrified.
This time, I no longer asked them to wait for another discovery.
We planned to leave when we were close enough to be noticed by the other vessel.
But Nemo kept the warship following him, drawing it onward through the night.
Before we could act, the Nautilus went down.
Through the window, I saw the great dark hull above us.
Our own vessel moved toward its unprotected underside.
There was a terrible shock.
The Nautilus passed through, and water poured into the broken ship.
It sank before our eyes, carrying the people aboard into the deep.
I could not turn the sea back or close the wound in that hull.
I could only look away.
Nemo watched until the last shape disappeared.
Then he went into his room.
From the doorway, I saw him kneel before the pictures of a woman and two children.
His shoulders shook, and his grief made no sound that could explain what I had seen.
Who was this man, and what had driven him to such a life?
The answers remained behind the closed door.

The Maelstrom
After that night, I saw little of the captain.
The Nautilus moved north, and the familiar life inside it seemed to fade.
Ned watched for a coast, while Conseil gathered my notes.
We no longer spoke of remaining aboard.
One evening, Ned came to me with news: we were near land, and he meant to leave that night.
The small boat was fitted into the submarine, and we could release it from inside.
It was a dangerous plan, but we had no better one.
I put my papers where I could carry them and waited for the appointed hour.
Each movement of the ship seemed louder than before.
I listened outside my cabin, afraid of meeting someone in the passage.
From Nemo's room came the deep notes of the organ.
The music rose and fell through the quiet vessel.
Then I saw the captain moving slowly in the darkness.
He seemed to know nothing of me or of the escape being prepared.
I heard him speak a few broken words of anguish, and hurried away.
Ned and Conseil were waiting in the boat.
We began to loosen its attachments while the Nautilus rolled beneath us.
Suddenly, voices rang through the submarine.
One word reached us again and again: Maelstrom.
I knew the name of that terrible whirlpool near Norway.
Strong currents rushed around it and could drag a vessel into its turning center.
The Nautilus had been caught.
Our boat shook violently before we had finished freeing it.
Ned struggled with the last fastening, and Conseil held me against the seat.
There was a crash, and I struck my head.
The boat seemed to tear away, but I remember no more.
When I opened my eyes, I lay in a fisherman's house on the Norwegian coast.
Ned and Conseil were safe beside me.
The whirlpool had thrown our boat clear, though I cannot tell how we survived it.
For many days, I rested and put my notes in order.
Our voyage had carried us twenty thousand leagues through the oceans of the world.
That was the distance of our journey, not a depth beneath the waves.
I had seen living forests, shining creatures, lost ruins, and the terrible country of ice.
But the greatest mystery of the voyage was still Captain Nemo.
Had the Nautilus escaped the Maelstrom?
Was its captain still traveling beneath the sea, beyond every nation's reach?
Some nights I could almost hear his organ through the water.
I could tell the world what I had discovered, but I could not finish his story.
