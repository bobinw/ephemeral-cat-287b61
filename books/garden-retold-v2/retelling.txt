The Child No One Looked For

Mary Lennox had learned to give orders before she learned to make friends.
In the hot house in India, servants brought what she wanted and hurried away before she could complain again.
Her father was busy, her mother preferred parties, and Mary grew thin and cross in the spaces between them.
She did not know that other children could be wanted, so she seldom thought of wanting anyone herself.
Then a terrible illness passed through the household.
People whispered, doors remained shut, and the servant Mary expected each morning did not come.
At first she was angry, then she found food for herself and hid in an empty room.
When men finally discovered her, both her parents were dead and the servants had died or fled.
She had been left alive in a house where no one had remembered to look for her.
She did not cry for the parents she had scarcely known, but the world had become more empty than she understood.
They sent her to England, to an uncle whose name meant nothing to her.

The House Across the Moor

Mrs. Medlock met Mary and spoke of Misselthwaite Manor as though it were a place too large for questions.
There were many rooms, most of them locked, and Mr. Craven did not wish to be disturbed.
His wife had died years before, and he spent much of his time away.
As their carriage crossed the moor at night, Mary saw only darkness stretching beyond the windows.
The wind sounded like a thing that had been traveling for a long time and still had nowhere to stop.
In her room the next morning, a young servant named Martha talked freely while lighting the fire.
Martha expected Mary to dress herself, which Mary considered an unreasonable demand.
In India, someone had always done it for her.
Martha was surprised rather than frightened by Mary's temper, and spoke warmly of her own crowded home.
Her brother Dickon could make friends with creatures that usually ran from people.
Mary listened, though she pretended not to care.
Martha advised her to go outside, where the air might do her more good than staring at the walls.
Mary did not become pleasant merely because she had crossed an ocean.
She disliked the unfamiliar food, disliked being left alone, and disliked hearing that another child could amuse himself.
Martha sometimes spoke more freely than a servant in India would have dared, and Mary discovered that anger did not always produce obedience.
The discovery was uncomfortable, but it gave the room another person's shape instead of making everything an extension of Mary's wishes.
Martha told stories of twelve children in a small house, of shared food, work, and a mother who understood them.
Mary had lived among more servants than that, yet had never known such company.
She asked questions in a voice that still sounded demanding, though curiosity was beginning to replace complaint.
Outside, the moor stretched farther than her old garden, with wind that did not care whether she liked it.
Walking against it required effort, and that effort gave her something to do besides dislike the day.
At meals she found herself eating food she would once have pushed away.
The changes were small enough to escape her attention, which is often how a child begins changing.

A Robin With Opinions

The gardens were full of winter branches, and Mary walked through them with her hands in her pockets.
A robin appeared near an old gardener named Ben Weatherstaff.
The bird tilted its head, looked at Mary, and seemed entirely willing to judge her for itself.
She spoke to it, and was surprised to hear friendliness in her own voice.
Ben said the robin liked company, a taste that neither he nor Mary was skilled at satisfying.
He observed that they both had sour faces, and Mary had never heard herself described so plainly.
She found a long wall covered in ivy and wondered about the garden behind it.
Martha told her that Mrs. Craven had loved it, and that a branch had broken while she sat beneath a tree.
After his wife died from her injuries, Mr. Craven locked the gate and buried its key.
No one was to enter, and the garden had been shut for ten years.
Mary began looking at the wall as though it were keeping a secret specially from her.
For the first time in a long while, she wanted something that a servant could not simply bring.

The Key Beneath the Earth

Martha's mother sent Mary a skipping rope, bought with money that her family could scarcely spare.
Mary had received more costly presents, but none had made her feel quite like this one.
She learned to skip, grew warmer, and began to notice that she was hungry at mealtimes.
One day the robin hopped near a patch of freshly disturbed earth, and she saw a metal ring.
She dug out a rusty key and held it as though it were a message.
Later a gust lifted the ivy along the wall, revealing the outline of a hidden door.
The key turned, the door moved, and Mary stepped into the silence of ten years.
Rose branches climbed over old trees, and gray stems tangled above beds covered in grass.
Everything looked dead until she noticed small green points coming through the soil.
She knelt and cleared the grass around them, afraid of hurting something that had begun so quietly.
The garden was not waiting for someone to order it alive.
It had already started, and Mary could help.
Mary wanted tools and seeds, but asking for them raised the danger of being asked what she intended to grow.
She arranged for Martha to ask Dickon, using the small allowance that had been given to her.
Each choice mattered because she had begun to imagine something becoming alive through her own work.
The robin came while she worked and stood close enough to seem like an interested supervisor.
She cleared a little more ground each day, stopping when she was uncertain rather than pulling everything out.
Ben answered her questions about roses without guessing the true reason for them.
He was rough in speech, but careful in the way he handled growing things.
Mary noticed this contradiction and found that a person's face was not the whole story of that person.
The thought mattered to a child who had been judged disagreeable before anyone asked why.
As the green points rose, she looked forward to mornings instead of waiting for someone to make them bearable.
The wall still kept the garden hidden, but inside it Mary was becoming less hidden from herself.

A Boy Who Could Keep a Secret

Dickon arrived with seeds and tools, carrying the smell of the moor into the garden.
A crow and a fox were among his companions, and Mary watched them as carefully as she watched him.
He did not speak as though animals were toys, or as though a garden existed only to obey a gardener.
Mary showed him the hidden door after asking if he could keep a secret.
He promised, and she believed him because he listened without trying to own what she was telling.
Inside, he bent the rose branches gently and showed her the living green beneath their bark.
Some growth was dead, some sleeping, and only patient attention could tell the difference.
They worked side by side, speaking softly as though loud voices might frighten the new leaves.
Mary's hands grew dirty, her face took on color, and she forgot to ask what anyone would do for her.
When she met her uncle briefly, she asked for a little piece of earth.
He gave permission without guessing which piece already filled her thoughts.

Crying Behind the Walls

At night, Mary heard crying through the house, and the explanations she received did not satisfy her.
One stormy night she followed it through corridors until she opened a door on a boy in bed.
He stared as if she were a dream, and she stared because he was a secret larger than the garden.
His name was Colin Craven, and he was her cousin.
He believed that he would die young or grow a crooked back, and everyone moved carefully around his fears.
His father seldom came, and Colin mistook that painful absence for proof that he should not have been born.
Mary spoke of the moor, Dickon, and growing things, and he listened as if she had brought weather into his room.
He was used to obedience and threatened to keep her beside him whenever he wished.
Mary, who knew something about selfishness, disliked meeting it in another person.
They argued fiercely, then discovered that an argument could end without either person disappearing.
That was an unusual discovery for two children who had spent so much time alone.

Beyond the Bedroom

One night Colin screamed that he could feel a lump on his back.
Mary became angry with his certainty of disaster and insisted on looking.
She found no lump, only the sharp bones of a thin child, and told him what she saw.
His crying quieted when someone finally answered the fear instead of moving politely around it.
Mary told him the garden secret, and Colin began to want a future long enough to see it.
Dickon visited with his animals, and soon a plan was made to take Colin outside in a chair.
They chose paths where few servants would see them, because Colin wanted the change to remain their secret.
When the hidden door opened, spring was waiting with color, scent, and movements too small to command.
Colin looked at the roses, the trees, and the wide blue above them, and forgot for a while to inspect his own body.
The garden gave him more to notice than the question of whether he was dying.
Colin's first visit had required more planning than the servants imagined when they saw the chair taken outside.
He could command people to stay away, but could not command himself to stop fearing every unusual sensation.
Mary and Dickon helped by giving his attention places to go.
They showed him birds, new shoots, and small animals that had more urgent business than discussing his health.
He began asking about the garden as he had once asked about his symptoms.
The questions did not cure every fear, but they filled part of the day with something other than fear.
Mary sometimes forgot that she had been sent away from one lonely house into another.
She was needed now, not merely attended, and the difference could be felt whenever Colin waited for her story.
Dickon moved easily between them, bringing the plain habits of a family that expected children to help as well as receive help.
The garden belonged to none of them in the way a toy belongs to its owner.
They could share its work, watch its changes, and find themselves changing alongside it.

Standing in the Garden

Ben Weatherstaff saw them over the wall and was astonished by the boy in the chair.
He spoke bluntly, as he always did, and called Colin the poor crippled lad.
The words angered Colin so deeply that he demanded help to stand.
Dickon supported him, and the thin legs held his weight while Ben stared.
It was not an easy walk, nor the end of all difficulty, but it was a beginning he could feel.
Ben admitted that he had secretly cared for the roses because he had loved Colin's mother.
The secret now had another keeper, and the garden gained another pair of working hands.
Colin began speaking of Magic, by which he meant the mysterious energy pushing leaves from branches and helping him grow.
He practiced standing and walking, ate more, and learned that wanting strength included work as well as words.
Sometimes the children had to hide their hungry appetites so that the household would not discover their progress.
Dickon's mother helped with simple food and a kindness that made room for all three of them.
They still had tempers and secrets, but their days were no longer built entirely from loneliness.

The Door Opens for a Father

Far from home, Mr. Craven traveled through beautiful places without letting their beauty reach him.
Grief had become the country he carried wherever he went.
Gradually he found moments of peace, and one night dreamed that his wife called him into the garden.
A letter from Dickon's mother asked him to return, and he decided to go home.
At Misselthwaite he walked toward the locked garden, expecting the same wall and the same ache.
Instead he heard children's voices and movement behind the ivy.
The door opened, and a boy ran out and almost fell into his arms.
For a moment he did not recognize the strong, bright face of his own son.
Colin brought him inside and told him about Mary, Dickon, Ben, and the secret days that had changed everything.
His father looked at the garden where he had shut away his sorrow, and found it full of life.
When they crossed the lawn together, the servants gathered at the windows in amazement.
Colin walked beside his father with his head lifted, and the old house had something new to look at.
Behind them, the door to the garden no longer needed to keep everyone outside.
