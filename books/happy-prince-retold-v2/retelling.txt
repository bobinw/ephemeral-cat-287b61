A Beautiful View

Above the city stood a prince who never moved, dressed in thin gold and watched by everyone passing below.
His eyes were blue sapphires, and a red ruby shone on the handle of his sword.
A councillor praised his beauty, then hurried to add that he was not very useful.
The man liked to appear wise about art, but not foolish about money.
A mother pointed to the statue as an example for her crying child, because the Happy Prince never complained.
No one thought to ask whether standing above the whole city might give a person something to cry about.
One evening a Swallow arrived, late for his journey to Egypt.
He had stayed beside a reed all summer, admiring her graceful movements and expecting her to travel with him.
But the reed belonged to the river, and she could bow beautifully without ever packing for a journey.
The disappointed bird had finally left, and now chose the space between the Prince's feet as his bedroom.
He admired the gold, tucked his head beneath his wing, and felt a large drop fall on his back.
There were no clouds, which made the weather seem especially unreasonable.
Then he looked up and saw tears on the golden face.

The Palace Wall

The Prince told the Swallow that when he was alive, people had called him happy.
He had lived in a palace where music and pleasure filled the days and sorrow was never invited.
A high wall surrounded the garden, and he had never asked what lay beyond it.
Now he stood so high that he could see suffering in streets he had never walked.
His heart was made of lead, but the sight still made him weep.
Across the city, a woman was sewing fine flowers onto a gown while her sick child lay in bed.
The boy wanted oranges, but she had only water to give him.
The gown was for someone at court, where beauty could be admired without seeing the tired hands that made it.
The Prince asked the Swallow to take the ruby from his sword to the woman.
The Swallow said his friends were waiting in Egypt, where the river was warm and the flowers were opening.
He also said that boys had thrown stones at him, which made helping one seem less attractive.
But the Prince's voice was so sad that the Swallow agreed to remain for one night.

A Night Made Warm

The Swallow carried the ruby across the city and looked through the poor woman's window.
She had fallen asleep from exhaustion, while the child tossed in his hot bed.
The bird placed the jewel beside her sewing things and flew gently around the boy's face.
His wings stirred the air, and the boy felt cool enough to sleep.
On the way, the Swallow had passed bright windows where people spoke carelessly about the gown being late.
From outside, the distance between those rooms and the sewing woman's room seemed larger than any street.
He returned to the Prince and said he felt warm, although the night was cold.
The Prince told him that helping someone had warmed him.
The Swallow considered this idea, then went to sleep before thinking made him too tired.
In the morning he visited the river, washed, and announced his departure as though the matter were quite settled.
But when evening came, the Prince asked for another night.

The Eyes of the Prince

In an attic, a young writer sat over pages he could not finish because cold had stiffened his hands.
The room had no fire, and hunger left him too weak to work.
The Prince had no ruby left, so he asked the Swallow to take one of his sapphire eyes.
The bird protested, but at last pulled out the stone and carried it through a hole in the roof.
The young man found it and imagined that someone who admired his work had sent him help.
He could buy food and wood, and his unfinished story could continue.
The next evening the Prince saw a little match girl whose matches had fallen into the water.
She had earned nothing and feared a beating when she went home.
He asked the Swallow to take his other eye to her.
The Swallow cried out that the Prince would then be blind, but the request did not change.
He dropped the sapphire into the girl's hand, and she ran home rejoicing over what she thought was a wonderful piece of glass.
Returning to the statue, the bird looked at the face that could no longer see him.
“I will stay,” he said, and this time he did not promise only one night.

Stories From Below

The Swallow told the blind Prince about Egypt, where strange birds moved beside the river and old stone figures watched the desert.
He spoke of distant wonders as though he could lend the Prince a little of his own sight.
The Prince listened, then asked him to fly over this city and describe what was happening there.
The bird saw people feasting behind warm windows and children shivering outside them.
Under a bridge, two hungry boys held each other for warmth until a guard drove them into the rain.
The Swallow carried these sights back, and the Prince told him to remove the gold covering his body.
Leaf by leaf, the bright surface vanished, and leaf by leaf the city received small chances to live.
Children bought bread and laughed again, while the statue became dull and gray.
Those below could not see why it had changed, and those who praised its beauty had no habit of looking farther.
Winter came, and the Swallow grew cold, keeping himself alive with crumbs when he could find them.
He stayed near the Prince's shoulder, which had become dearer to him than the warm country he described.

The Journey at Last

At last the Swallow knew that his strength was nearly gone.
He flew once more to the Prince's shoulder and asked to kiss his hand.
The Prince thought he was finally leaving for Egypt and said he should go before the cold grew worse.
But the bird was going on a different journey, one from which travelers did not return.
He kissed the Prince, then fell dead at his feet.
Inside the statue, the lead heart broke in two with a sharp sound.
The next morning the Mayor walked past with his councillors and noticed how shabby the statue had become.
It had lost its jewels and gold, so they agreed that it was no longer suitable for the city.
They also noticed the dead bird, though no one asked why it had remained there.
The statue was pulled down, and the metal was melted while important men argued over whose image should replace it.
The broken lead heart would not melt, and the workers threw it onto a rubbish heap beside the Swallow.
Then God asked an angel to bring the two most precious things in the city.
The angel brought the broken heart and the dead bird.
What the city had discarded was welcomed elsewhere, and the Prince and his companion were no longer cold.
