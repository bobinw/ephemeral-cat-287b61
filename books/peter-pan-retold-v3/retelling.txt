The Boy at the Window
The Darling children had a nurse who walked on four legs.
Her name was Nana, and she took her work very seriously.
She checked bath water, carried forgotten shoes, and watched the bedroom as if no danger in London could get past her nose.
Mr. Darling sometimes complained that a dog was an unusual nurse.
But when Nana gave him a disappointed look, he found it surprisingly difficult to argue.
Wendy, John, and Michael thought this was quite a sensible arrangement.
Their mother came upstairs every evening, bringing the calm of the rest of the house with her.
She listened to their stories and left the window a little open.
That was how Peter Pan first slipped into their lives.
Wendy spoke of him as if everybody ought to know him.
He lived far away, could fly without a machine, and had no intention of becoming a grown-up.
Mrs. Darling was less certain that a boy could be all these things.
Then one evening she saw him at the window herself.
Nana rushed at the stranger, the window shut, and Peter escaped without his shadow.
Mrs. Darling folded the shadow away in a drawer, as carefully as if it were a good winter coat.
A few nights later, after the parents had gone out, something bright began darting around the sleeping children's room.
It flashed inside a cupboard, behind a curtain, and into a jug.
This was Tinker Bell, a fairy small enough to hide in a hand.
She made a sound like tiny silver bells when she spoke.
Peter climbed through the window and found his shadow.
He pressed it against his feet, expecting the two things to join at once.
They did not.
He tried soap, which made his feet slippery and his shadow no more obedient.
Finally he sat down and cried.
Wendy woke and leaned over the edge of her bed.
"Have you lost something?" she asked.
Peter looked up, ashamed to be caught crying.
He said his name grandly, as if names could repair shadows.
Wendy offered to sew it back on.
"Will it hurt?" Peter asked, trying to sound as if pain were a subject he had never needed to consider.
"I shall be careful," Wendy promised.
"Then I shall not cry," he said, though his wet face suggested that this was a new decision.
She fetched her needle, and Peter held still with great difficulty.
When she finished, he sprang up and danced around the room.
"Look what I can do!" he cried.
He seemed to have forgotten that Wendy had done most of the work.
She turned toward the wall, hurt by his careless pride.
Peter came closer and declared that girls were worth far more than boys, which was a very convenient discovery just then.
Wendy offered him a kiss; Peter held out his hand to receive it.
Not wishing to embarrass him, she put a thimble into his palm, and he gave her an acorn button in return.
Wendy hung the button on a chain near her heart, calling it his kiss.
Peter told her about Neverland, where the Lost Boys waited for him beneath the trees.
They knew how to hunt, hide, and fight pirates, but nobody knew how to finish a bedtime story.
Wendy could scarcely believe such a serious problem existed.
"You could tell us stories," Peter suggested.
Tinker Bell rang furiously inside the drawer where he had accidentally shut her.
He let her out, but her angry little light stayed close to Wendy's face.
Wendy looked at her sleeping brothers, then at the wide night beyond the window.
An ordinary bedroom had suddenly become the edge of an extraordinary world.

Above the Sleeping World
Wendy wanted to go, but an adventure was no excuse for leaving John and Michael behind.
She shook them awake, and John put on his hat immediately.
He felt that a gentleman ought to be properly dressed when leaving by a window.
Michael asked whether they would be back before breakfast.
Peter had very little interest in breakfast times.
First, he explained, they must think of something wonderfully happy.
The children tried, jumped from their beds, and landed on the floor with three heavy bumps.
Peter laughed until Wendy asked whether he had forgotten anything.
He had forgotten the fairy dust.
"You might have mentioned that," John said, rubbing his elbow.
"I am mentioning it now," Peter replied cheerfully.
Wendy decided that arguing with him would use up the entire night.
Once he sprinkled it over them, their feet began behaving like balloons.
Michael floated toward the ceiling and discovered that coming down was not as easy as going up.
John attempted a careful turn and flew straight into a curtain.
Wendy reached for the bedpost, frightened by the beautiful impossibility of it all.
Then she let go.
They sailed through the window into the cool London air.
Below them, street lamps stretched into shining lines, and roofs fitted together like pieces of a dark puzzle.
Nana barked from the garden, pulling at the chain that kept her there.
For a moment Wendy wanted to turn back and explain everything.
But Peter was already ahead, and the city was falling away behind them.
They flew over fields, rivers, and the black shape of the sea.
Peter pointed into the sky and gave directions that sounded easier to say than to follow.
Stars did not look much like street corners.
John tried asking how far they had to go.
Peter gave an answer, changed it, and then decided that distance was not an interesting subject.
Sometimes he flew away to inspect something, forgetting that his new companions could not manage quite so well.
When Michael grew sleepy, he began to sink.
Peter swept down after him, catching him just before the ocean could.
To Peter, it was a splendid game.
Wendy caught Michael by the sleeve and kept him closer after that.
At last an island rose out of the blue distance.
Its forests, mountains, and shining water felt strangely familiar, like the shape of a place remembered from dreams.
Here were the paths they had imagined while lying in bed.
Here, too, were dangers that had seemed much smaller in those dreams.
Peter's voice changed as he looked down.
He pointed out the pirates' ship and warned them to fly quietly.
The pirates were watching the sky.
A sudden shot split the air, scattering the children.
Wendy lost sight of her brothers in the smoke and noise.
She followed Tinker Bell's bright light toward the forest, grateful for a guide.
But the fairy had become fierce with jealousy.
Below them, the Lost Boys looked up and saw a white figure moving between the branches.
Tinker Bell rang out a message, pretending it was Peter's order.
The boys reached for their bows.
Wendy, still thinking she had found help, began to come down.
An arrow flew toward her.

The Little House in the Woods
Tootles was the boy who shot the arrow.
He was usually the one who missed the most exciting adventures.
This time, he thought, he would have something important to tell Peter.
Then Wendy fell, and the boys gathered around her in terrible silence.
She was not a bird after all.
She was a girl, and the discovery made their proud little victory turn cold.
Peter arrived carrying a promise of wonderful news.
He had brought them a storyteller, perhaps even someone who would care for them like a mother.
Tootles looked from Peter to the still figure on the ground.
"I shot her," he said.
He did not run away or try to blame another boy.
Peter's face hardened, and he raised a weapon in anger.
Then Wendy's hand moved.
The arrow had struck the acorn button hanging on her chain, instead of entering her chest.
The little gift had saved her life.
Peter put down his weapon, and the boys breathed again.
Tinker Bell's secret did not stay hidden for long.
Peter banished her for what she had done, though Wendy, even while weak, asked him to soften the punishment.
Peter first announced that Tinker Bell must go away forever, then reduced forever to a week.
Now the boys faced another problem.
They had a girl lying in the forest, and waking her seemed terribly rude.
It was decided that they should build a house around her.
The plan suited Neverland perfectly.
The boys ran for branches, leaves, and everything else that might persuade a house to exist.
Peter directed the work with the confidence of someone who had never paid a builder.
When Wendy opened her eyes, a small doorway waited before her.
A roof of leaves stood above her, and the boys were trying to look like respectful visitors.
They asked her to be their mother.
Wendy reminded them that she was only a little girl.
They said that this was much better than having no mother at all.
It was difficult to refuse people who made such hopeful faces.
"You must promise to listen," she said.
The boys promised at once, which was easy because they had not yet discovered how much listening might be required.
John and Michael soon arrived, dusty, excited, and full of questions about the arrows.
They were pleased to find Wendy alive, but less pleased to discover that she had become everybody's mother while they were away.
Peter showed them the way to the underground home.
Each boy had his own hollow tree, with a passage narrow enough to fit his body.
A person who ate too much might find getting home a serious problem.
Wendy looked at the dark opening and imagined herself stuck halfway down, with dinner waiting below.
The boys assured her that they could make a door especially for her.
Inside, a fire glowed, rough beds lined the walls, and the island's busy noises sounded far away.
Wendy felt the first warm pleasure of being needed.
She did not yet know how much work everybody's mother would have to do.
Above the roof of earth, pirates moved between the trees.

Days That Felt Like Dreams
Life under the ground soon became a mixture of adventure and housework.
Wendy discovered holes in clothes, missing buttons, and boys who believed washing was a punishment invented elsewhere.
She washed, mended, cooked, and tried to remember which child had last had a proper bath.
Peter acted as the father of their pretend family whenever the idea amused him.
At other times he disappeared into the forest, returning with a story nobody could entirely check.
It was not always clear whether his adventures had happened or whether he had simply decided they ought to have happened.
In Neverland, the difference sometimes seemed unusually small.
The children held meals at the underground table.
When there was real food, they ate real food.
When Peter preferred pretending, everyone had to pretend with him, and hungry stomachs were expected to be good sports.
"May I have another helping?" John asked once, holding out his empty plate.
"You have had three already," Peter told him.
John looked at Wendy, hoping that somewhere in the family there was a person who understood the difference between a meal and an idea.
John liked making rules for games.
Michael liked changing them just before he lost.
The Lost Boys argued about turns, places, and whose story Wendy should tell next.
She could stop a quarrel more easily with the promise of a story than with a command.
At bedtime, they pressed close to hear her voice.
The fire made shadows climb the walls, and for a little while even Peter sat quietly.
Wendy told them about a family in London with a dog for a nurse.
Michael listened as if the dog were an interesting invention.
That troubled her.
She began asking John and Michael questions about home.
What did their father look like, and where did their mother sit in the evening?
John answered confidently, then paused when she asked for details.
Michael remembered some things beautifully and other things not at all.
Wendy turned remembering into a kind of lesson, afraid that a place might vanish from their hearts if they stopped visiting it in their thoughts.
Outside, the island offered a different lesson each day.
They followed animal tracks through the wood, listened for footsteps, and learned which silences meant somebody was hiding.
Sometimes they saw mermaids lying in the sunlight by the lagoon.
Wendy wished the mermaids would speak kindly to her, but they had no desire to welcome another girl.
They laughed and splashed, showing bright tails before sliding away.
The children played near the water until its pleasant surface began to darken.
Neverland was not equally friendly at every hour.
As evening came, the lagoon seemed to keep secrets beneath its smooth skin.
Wendy noticed that Peter listened more carefully there.
He might forget meals, promises, and the names of old friends.
But he could hear danger moving in a place that looked empty.
One afternoon, while the others rested by the water, he lifted his head.
A boat was approaching.
It carried pirates and a prisoner who was trying very hard not to show fear.
Peter's smile sharpened.
For him, the day had finally become interesting.

The Captain Who Heard a Clock
Captain Hook had a fine coat, careful manners, and a metal hook where one hand should have been.
He used the hook so easily that the pirates often forgot how frightening it looked to newcomers.
They never forgot for long.
Hook could speak softly while planning something cruel, which made his softness unpleasant rather than comforting.
He commanded his ship as if every rope and sailor owed him obedience.
Yet there was one sound that could turn this proud captain pale.
Long ago, Peter had cut off his hand and thrown it to a crocodile.
The animal enjoyed the meal and followed Hook, hoping for the rest of him.
It had also swallowed a clock.
Whenever Hook heard ticking nearby, he knew its great jaws were coming closer.
The pirates could see him listening even when he pretended not to be afraid.
Smee, who wore spectacles and looked less dangerous than he was, tried to comfort him.
If the clock ever stopped, he pointed out, the crocodile might approach without a warning.
This was not the sort of comfort Hook wanted.
"Do you think that makes the matter better?" the captain asked.
Smee considered the question and began polishing his spectacles.
It was safer to clean something than to answer too quickly.
What troubled the captain almost as much as the crocodile was Peter himself.
Peter had no interest in behaving like a proper enemy.
He laughed, flew, and treated Hook's serious plans as invitations to a game.
Worst of all, Peter seemed perfectly pleased with himself, without asking anyone whether he ought to be.
Hook called it good form: the proper way to behave, especially when someone else was watching.
But if he thought too hard about looking proper, was that itself improper?
The question troubled him almost as much as Peter, and was considerably harder to stab.
Now Hook's pirates had captured Tiger Lily, the daughter of the island people's chief.
They brought her to a rock in the lagoon where the rising water would soon reach her.
She stood bound and silent, refusing to beg.
Hidden nearby, Wendy watched with Peter.
The game had become something much more serious.
Peter could imitate voices wonderfully well.
From his hiding place, he spoke in Hook's own tone and ordered the men to release their prisoner.
The pirates stopped, confused.
They thought their captain had changed his mind, and obeying seemed safer than asking why.
Tiger Lily slipped away before they discovered the trick.
Then the real Hook appeared, and the lagoon filled with angry voices.
Peter answered from the shadows, enjoying the captain's confusion.
Hook looked around, unable to tell whether the voice belonged to a boy, a spirit, or his own fear.
The Lost Boys attacked the pirates, and the quiet water broke into splashes and shouts.
Wendy tried to find Peter among the moving figures.
Above the fighting, the sunlight faded.
Below it, the tide kept rising, with no interest in who won.

The Rock and the Rising Tide
Peter and Hook met on a slippery rock, close enough for Peter to see the captain's anger in his eyes.
Peter noticed that Hook stood lower than he did.
He offered him a hand to bring him up, because fighting from unequal positions did not seem fair.
Hook answered by biting the offered hand.
For a moment Peter was more astonished than hurt.
He had expected an enemy to fight, but the unfairness of the bite shocked him.
The pause gave Hook a chance to strike again.
Peter was wounded, and Wendy hurried toward him through the confusion.
Then the familiar ticking reached the captain's ears.
His face changed at once.
He fled, abandoning the fight, as the crocodile moved through the darkening water.
The other children escaped with the boat, believing Wendy and Peter would follow.
Instead, the two were left on the rock.
Water climbed around their feet.
Peter tried to fly, but he was too weak to carry Wendy, and the rising tide gave them little time.
Wendy looked at the space between the rock and the shore.
It had seemed a short distance in the afternoon.
Now it looked like a different country.
A kite drifted toward them, tugging at a loose string.
Peter caught it and tied the string around Wendy.
It could carry one person to safety, he said.
Wendy wanted him to come too.
"We could hold on together," she said.
Peter tested the pull of the string and shook his head.
"It would not carry us both," he answered, and for once there was no room in his voice for pretending.
He shook his head and sent her away before she could keep arguing.
The wind lifted her above the water, taking her toward the waiting boys.
Peter remained alone.
The rock became smaller and smaller beneath him.
For once, there was nobody to admire his courage and nobody to laugh at his jokes.
He stood straight, and the thought of death came to him wearing the bright clothes of a new adventure.
Then a bird came toward him, pushing a nest across the water.
Her eggs lay inside it.
The nest had been washed away, and she had spent a long time trying to keep it safe.
Now she was offering it to the boy.
Peter and the bird could not understand each other's speech.
She shouted in bird language; he replied with great confidence in his own.
Both thought the other was behaving rather foolishly.
At last he understood the important part without understanding a single word.
The nest could hold him.
He would not leave her eggs in the water, so he moved them into a floating hat.
The bird settled on the hat, and Peter climbed into the nest.
They floated away, two small boats sharing the same escape.
When Peter returned to the underground home, Wendy threw her arms around him.
The boys cheered and demanded every detail.
Peter enjoyed telling it, but the nest in the dark had taught him something his proud voice did not quite explain.
For a little while, his life had depended on an ordinary bird deciding to help.

A Story That Led Home
After Peter saved Tiger Lily, her people helped guard the underground home.
The children felt safer, and the days continued in their bright, untidy fashion.
Wendy still had washing to do, even when everybody else had been fighting pirates.
She wondered whether mothers ever received a day off after an adventure.
One evening, she gathered the boys around the fire and began a familiar story.
There had once been three children, she told them, who flew away from their parents.
Their mother had loved them, and their father had loved them too, although fathers sometimes showed it badly.
The children visited a wonderful island and stayed away for a long time.
At first they remembered every part of home.
Then the smaller memories began to fade.
Wendy paused, watching John and Michael.
Michael leaned against her, listening with the calm of a child who expected the world to look after him.
In her story, the mother kept a window open, waiting for the children to return.
Wendy said this with absolute confidence.
Whatever happened, surely the window would still be open.
Peter interrupted.
He said mothers did not always wait.
He had once gone back to his own window, he explained, and found it closed.
Another child had taken his place.
His voice was hard and casual, but the room seemed colder after he spoke.
Wendy could not tell how much he remembered clearly and how much belonged to his hurt.
She could hear that something in the memory frightened him.
And suddenly it frightened her too.
What if her mother was waiting now, while she sat beneath another island telling stories?
What if the window were already shut, and Michael had no bed to return to?
She stood and began finding her brothers' things.
They must leave for London at once.
John agreed, though he wished the decision had been made on a day with less promising weather for adventure.
Michael asked whether their mother would know them.
That question made Wendy pack faster.
The Lost Boys watched her in dismay.
They asked who would tell stories and remember their bedtime rules.
Wendy invited them to come too.
Perhaps her parents could find room for children who had no home waiting for them.
The boys began gathering their belongings with sudden hope.
Peter laughed too loudly and said he was glad to have fewer people crowding the place.
Wendy knew that laughter could hide more than amusement.
She asked him to come with them.
He refused at once.
"You would not have to be grown-up straight away," Wendy said.
"I would have to begin," Peter answered.
She could not deny it, and he looked almost pleased to have found the part of her promise that frightened him.
Going home meant growing up, and Peter would not agree to either.
Their pretend family had reached the edge of its pretend life.
Wendy folded Michael's things, while Peter took out his pipes.
He played as merrily as he could, and Wendy wished he would stop.
Above them, the guards had begun listening to a silence that did not feel right.
Hook's men were moving through the forest.

The Light That Would Not Go Out
The pirates did not follow the familiar rules of the island's battles.
Hook attacked before the guards expected him, and the sudden fight swept through the forest.
Down below, the children heard the noise but could not see what was happening.
Then a signal sounded above them.
Peter believed it meant that their friends had won.
He sent Wendy and the boys up through the trees, sure that the paths were safe.
But the pirates had taken the signal for themselves.
One by one, the children climbed out and were seized before they could warn those below.
Michael was carried away with the others.
Wendy struggled to look back, hoping Peter would notice that nobody had called goodbye.
The pirates dragged them toward the ship.
Peter, left alone, lay down to sleep.
Hook found a way into the underground room and stood over his enemy.
Sleeping Peter looked much smaller than the laughing boy the captain knew.
That only made Hook hate him more.
Near the bed stood a cup of medicine Wendy had left for Peter.
Hook added poison to it, then slipped away.
He expected the boy to do the rest without knowing there was a battle left to fight.
Tinker Bell reached Peter after escaping the pirates' notice.
She rang urgently beside his head until he woke.
At first he was annoyed that she would not let him sleep.
Then he understood that Wendy and all the boys had been taken.
He sprang up, ready to fly after them.
But before leaving, he remembered Wendy's medicine and reached for the cup.
Tinker Bell darted between his hand and his mouth.
She told him it was poisoned.
Peter would not believe that Hook had entered his home without his noticing.
His pride made him trust his own certainty more than her warning.
There was no time for another argument.
Tinker Bell drank the medicine herself before he could take it.
"Why would you do that?" Peter cried.
Her answering bells were so faint that he had to bend close to hear them.
All his clever tricks seemed useless beside a light that might go out.
Her light began to weaken.
Peter caught her, suddenly frightened by how little she weighed.
Tinker Bell tried to ring crossly at him, but even being cross had become an effort.
Now he could not command her light to burn brighter.
He called out to children beyond the island, asking them to show that they believed in fairies.
Listen carefully: perhaps you can hear him too.
If you believe in fairies, clap your hands; he is waiting for you.
It grew louder, and Tinker Bell's light slowly strengthened.
Peter laughed, and Tinker Bell rang loudly enough to scold him again.
She was quite ready to be troublesome again, which was the best possible news.
The room still held the empty cup, a small ordinary object that had almost ended everything.
Peter snatched up his sword; Hook was going to have a very unpleasant night.
He flew into the night, following the way to Hook's ship.

Footsteps on the Pirate Ship
The ship lay dark on the water, with lanterns swinging above its deck.
Wendy and the boys had been tied up while Hook prepared their punishment.
The captain offered the boys a chance to join his crew.
Some looked at the pirates' weapons and wondered whether refusing would be the last brave thing they did.
Hook noticed their hesitation and smiled.
"There is room for useful boys aboard my ship," he said.
Wendy looked at the boys, hoping they could hear the threat hidden inside that invitation.
But they would not leave one another to face the captain alone.
Wendy stood straight, although she was frightened.
Hook disliked seeing children look at him without admiration.
He spoke of making them walk the plank, a narrow board stretched over the sea.
Below it, the water waited without making a promise.
The pirates gathered around, pleased to have an entertainment that did not put themselves in danger.
Then a sound reached the ship.
Tick, tick, tick.
Hook froze.
His fine words disappeared, and he hurried away from the rail.
The pirates stared into the dark, expecting the crocodile's eyes to appear.
But Peter had noticed that the animal's clock had stopped.
He made the ticking sound himself as he approached the ship.
What frightened Hook protected Peter better than a shield.
He climbed aboard while the captain hid, and slipped into the dark below the deck.
One pirate was sent down and did not come back.
Another went after him.
The ship's noisy confidence began to break apart.
When a strange crowing sound rose from below, the pirates decided something unnatural had come among them.
Hook ordered men forward, but orders did not make their feet move faster.
Peter used the confusion to reach the prisoners.
Wendy recognized him before the others did, though he kept his voice low and his movements quick.
He cut the ropes around the boys and told them where to find weapons.
For once they followed his instructions without demanding to know the whole plan.
John steadied Michael, who was trying to look taller than he was.
The Lost Boys spread out, with swords that suddenly felt much heavier than they had in games.
Wendy stayed close, watching for anyone who might be left behind.
Peter left her in a place where the pirates expected to find a prisoner.
He used a cloak to hide his own shape for the next part of the trick.
Hook returned to the deck, determined to prove that he was still captain of this ship.
But the children were free, the darkness had become an ally, and his men no longer knew whom they should fear most.
A fight broke out before Hook could understand how completely the night had changed.
The boys fought for their lives and for the way home.
Peter fought with the sharp delight of someone who had finally found his favorite game.
He crowed, and Wendy recognized the sound even above the clash of swords.
Across the deck, Hook saw the boy he hated.
This time there was no forest, rock, or secret voice between them.
They faced each other beneath the swinging lamps.

A Captain's Last Battle
Hook's sword flashed, and Peter moved aside so lightly that he seemed hardly attached to the deck.
Around them, the fight continued, but the captain's eyes stayed fixed on the boy.
He wanted to end the laughter that had followed him through so many defeats.
Peter met his attacks quickly, using his small size and sudden movements.
Hook had strength, experience, and a terrible reach.
Peter had speed and the unsettling confidence of someone who could forget fear before it grew roots.
The captain tried to find a weakness in that confidence.
He demanded to know what kind of creature Peter really was.
Peter answered with the bright, careless certainty of youth.
He announced that he was youth itself, and joy, and a little bird newly out of its egg.
Hook had built his pride out of being a gentleman and a captain.
Before this boy, those titles seemed suddenly heavy.
Still, he fought hard.
His hook caught and tore, his sword struck, and Peter had to keep moving.
Once his sword knocked Hook's weapon away, and he stepped back to let the captain pick it up.
That courtesy hurt Hook more than a scratch: the boy had behaved properly without even thinking about it.
At last the fight pushed Hook toward the edge of the ship.
Beyond the rail, the sea looked black.
The crocodile was waiting there, with no clock left to announce it.
Hook saw that the end of his great fear had arrived without its familiar sound.
Even then, he wanted one last victory of manners.
He tried to make Peter behave badly, so that the captain could leave the world believing himself the better gentleman.
Hook invited a last kick, and Peter gave it without a moment's hesitation.
The captain went over the rail delighted by this final piece of bad manners.
The crocodile closed over the captain, and the water settled again.
For a little while, nobody spoke.
The sea closed over him; then Peter's crow rang across the deck.
Then the boys began to cheer.
The surviving pirates were beaten, and Smee managed to escape rather than share his captain's fate.
Peter took command of the ship with immediate pleasure.
He tried on the idea of being captain as easily as another boy might try on a hat.
Before long he was giving orders with some of Hook's grand manner.
Sometimes he stood with one arm crooked, and the boys looked at each other rather uneasily.
"Captain," she said, choosing the title because she knew it would please him.
Peter turned with a grand movement of his new coat.
"We still need to go home," she reminded him.
He sighed as if homes were forever getting in the way of important business.
She reminded Peter that they had a destination.
London was not a word to be put aside until another adventure had finished.
The boys set the ship toward home under his direction.
They climbed ropes, watched the sea, and slept wherever exhaustion found them.
Wendy checked on Michael and John, grateful each time she found them safe.
Wendy tucked a coat around Michael, who had fallen asleep in the middle of a victory.
There would be plenty to tell their mother, if they could remember it all.
Ahead of them lay an ordinary house, an open window, and people who had been waiting longer than the children understood.

The Window Still Open
In London, Mrs. Darling had kept the children's window open.
Cold nights came, and she covered the empty beds, but she would not close the way back.
Mr. Darling blamed himself for putting Nana outside on the night the children disappeared.
In his misery, he began sleeping in the dog's kennel.
It was an uncomfortable punishment, and quite impossible to explain properly to visitors.
Nana kept her watch, though there were no small feet to follow upstairs.
The house had all its usual rooms and almost none of its usual sounds.
Before Wendy and the others arrived, Peter flew ahead with Tinker Bell.
He found the open window and thought of the friends he was about to lose.
If the children could not get in, perhaps they would have to return to Neverland with him.
He shut the window.
For a moment, the plan seemed wonderfully simple.
Then he saw Mrs. Darling.
She sat close to the children's room, worn by waiting and still listening for sounds that did not come.
Peter looked at her and tried to make himself feel annoyed instead of troubled.
Why should a mother have Wendy when he wanted her too?
But the sight of her sadness would not leave him alone.
It followed him even when he turned his face away.
At last he opened the window again.
He muttered that he did not want a mother anyway, and left the window open.
He slipped away before the others could see what he had nearly done.
Wendy, John, and Michael came through the window into their old room.
Their beds looked familiar, but some details already felt strangely distant.
Michael wondered whether he had been there before.
Wendy felt a sharp ache at hearing the question.
He touched the bedpost and then looked at Nana, who needed no introduction.
They climbed into their beds, hoping to surprise their mother.
When Mrs. Darling entered, she looked at them and did not cry out.
She had dreamed of seeing them there so often that she thought this was another dream refusing to end.
Wendy called to her.
Then John called, and Michael reached out his arms.
Their mother's doubt broke, and she gathered them close.
The room filled with voices, tears, laughter, and Nana's delighted movements.
Mr. Darling emerged from the kennel, and the children found him easier to recognize when he was standing upright.
The Lost Boys waited nearby, afraid there might be no welcome left for them.
Wendy explained who they were.
Her parents opened their home to the boys, although finding enough beds would require more than a little thought.
Peter watched from outside, keeping himself beyond the warmth he had made possible.
Mrs. Darling invited him in too.
She offered care, a home, and the ordinary future of growing older.
Peter asked about school, work, and becoming a man.
"Would I have to sit at a desk?" he asked.
Mrs. Darling smiled and said that there would be many things to learn.
Peter had heard enough to suspect that even this kind woman could not offer a home without a future attached to it.
The answers made him step back.
He would not stay, but he asked whether Wendy could visit him sometimes.
An agreement was made for a short return to Neverland in the spring.
Wendy stood at the window while Peter disappeared into the sky.
She was happy to be home, and still sad that he could not be happy there beside her.

The Girl Who Grew Up
The Lost Boys learned the unfamiliar habits of ordinary life.
There were lessons to attend, clothes to keep tidy, and rules that did not change merely because Peter found them boring.
For a time, they could still fly.
Then daily life made that old skill seem less necessary, and gradually it left them.
John and Michael grew older beside them.
Wendy changed too, although the changes were so small each day that she did not always notice them.
Peter returned the first spring, and she went with him for a short visit.
She cleaned, mended, and listened to adventures as if no time had passed.
But time had passed for Wendy, even if Peter could not feel it.
He did not remember Captain Hook as clearly as she expected.
When she asked about Tinker Bell, his answer showed how quickly old companions slipped out of his mind.
He asked who she meant, then supposed that Tinker Bell must be dead: fairies did not live very long.
Wendy turned away for a moment; Peter was already asking for another story.
He had forgotten Hook too, though remembering a captain with a hook ought not to be very difficult.
Some springs he returned, and some he forgot.
Wendy waited, then learned to carry on with her own days.
School gave way to other responsibilities.
The boys became grown men, and Neverland moved into the place where childhood memories are kept.
Wendy married and had a daughter called Jane.
Jane loved hearing about the flying boy and the island beyond the night.
She listened from her bed, asking questions at exactly the places where Wendy used to ask them.
One evening, Peter appeared at the window again.
He came ready to take Wendy away, as if the years outside the room had been no longer than a game.
The woman waiting there was not the child he expected.
Wendy tried to explain gently.
She had grown up.
"You said you would come back," Peter said.
"I did come back," Wendy answered.
"But I could not keep being the same age while I waited."
Peter stared, searching her face for the girl he had left behind.
He had refused change so completely that he could not understand how she had let it happen.
For once, his proud little manner fell apart.
He cried.
Wendy could not make herself small again to spare him the pain.
He sat on the floor crying, just where he had once sat with his troublesome shadow.
Then Jane woke and saw the boy she knew from her mother's stories.
To her, he was not an ending.
He was the beginning of an adventure.
Peter lifted his head, and the lightness returned to his face.
With Wendy's permission, Jane went with him to Neverland for a visit.
Wendy went to the window and watched their two small shapes rise into the night.
Her sadness and her happiness stood side by side.
Later, Jane grew up too, and her daughter Margaret heard the same wonderful stories.
Peter came for Margaret too, except when he forgot, and she told him stories about himself.
He listened eagerly, as if nobody had ever had such remarkable adventures.
In London, one little girl grew taller while another began asking questions at bedtime.
Beyond the window, Peter was still the same small boy.
He would come again, with fairy dust and very little understanding of calendars.
There would be another open window, another story, and another child ready to fly.
And so it goes on, while children are merry, innocent, and wonderfully careless of the hearts they leave behind.
