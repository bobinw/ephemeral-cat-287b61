An Arrow That Could Not Return

In Sherwood, the leaves made a green roof above the paths, and the birds seemed to have no work except singing.
It was a fine place for a young man to forget that the world had laws, and Robin was eighteen.
He was walking to a shooting match with his bow on his shoulder when a group of royal foresters called him over.
They had been drinking, and a boy with a bow looked like an excellent subject for a joke.
“Can that little stick of yours send an arrow anywhere?” one asked.
Robin's cheeks burned, and he pointed to a deer far away among the trees.
They made a bet, and Robin shot it, forgetting in his anger that the king's deer belonged to the king.
The laughter stopped, and the men threatened to report him instead of paying.
Robin turned away, but an arrow flew so close that he heard it cut the air beside his head.
He turned, drew, and answered with an arrow of his own.
The man who had shot at him fell and did not rise again.
All the bright morning seemed to go dark around Robin.
An arrow could be picked up, but this one could never be called back.
He ran into the forest, and the Sheriff of Nottingham put a price on his head.
Other men with their own troubles joined him there, until Sherwood held a whole company of outlaws.
They lived beyond the law, yet poor people knew that a hungry visitor seldom left Robin's table hungry.
It would be pleasant to say that every man in the forest was wise and every officer outside it was foolish, but pleasant stories need not be true in every corner.
Robin had lost his home through pride and a moment of anger, and even his friends could not pretend otherwise.
In the greenwood, however, a man could begin again among others who knew what it was to be hunted.
They practiced shooting until the twang of a bowstring seemed as natural as birdsong.
They wrestled, tried their staffs, and disputed the results with an energy that would have impressed a judge.
When a traveler was brought to their table, Robin made certain that he ate before being questioned.
A greedy churchman might discover that his purse had grown lighter, while a poor workman might find a gift pressed into his hand.
Their judgments were their own, and the Sheriff would certainly have called them crimes.
Yet a farmer whose last coins had been taken by a powerful lord might not agree with the Sheriff.
So Robin's name traveled through cottages as warmly as it traveled through Nottingham's offices angrily.
The woods gave them room, but not safety, and someone always kept watch beyond the firelight.
When the feast was over, the men slept beneath the trees with their weapons near enough to reach in darkness.
It was a merry life with a hard edge, like a song sung by men who knew that the next verse might be interrupted.

A Very Large Little John

One morning Robin left his company behind and came to a narrow bridge across a stream.
A stranger was crossing from the other end, a man so tall that Robin had to look up to argue with him.
“Stand aside,” Robin said, as if saying it would make it happen.
“You have as much room to step aside as I have,” the stranger answered.
Robin lifted his bow, then saw that the other man carried only a staff.
He was proud, but not proud enough to enjoy such an unequal fight.
He cut himself a staff, returned to the bridge, and invited the stranger to settle the question properly.
Their sticks cracked together above the water, and neither was slow with his hands.
Robin landed a blow, but the stranger answered with one that sent him straight into the stream.
For a moment there was only splashing, followed by a very wet and rather surprised outlaw.
“Where are you now?” the stranger asked.
“In the water, plainly enough,” Robin said, and began to laugh.
He blew his horn, and armed men appeared among the trees, which made the stranger hold his staff more tightly.
But Robin told them that a man who could beat him fairly deserved a meal, not a beating.
The stranger was John Little, and the company decided that so enormous a man must be called Little John.
The name stuck, and so did the friendship.

A Tinker With a Heavy Staff

The Sheriff had soldiers, orders, and a great deal of patience for other people's failures.
What he did not have was Robin Hood.
A traveling tinker thought that catching one outlaw should be a simple way to earn a reward.
He met Robin on the road without knowing him and proudly showed the paper ordering his arrest.
Robin offered to help and led him to an inn, where advice was easy to give over a good drink.
When the tinker finally fell asleep, Robin took the paper and some money and slipped away.
It was a clever trick, but clever tricks sometimes come walking back after their owners.
The tinker woke furious, followed Robin, and challenged him with a staff that looked quite capable of breaking bones.
Robin took up another staff and soon discovered that this traveler was better at fighting than at guarding his pockets.
The blows grew heavier until Robin had to call for help.
Little John arrived, laughing at a leader who had caught a prize too large to carry.
The quarrel ended over food, and the tinker found that he liked the company he had come to arrest.
He stayed in Sherwood, where a strong arm was useful and a good joke could survive its teller's defeat.

A Rope in Nottingham

Not every adventure ended with a feast.
Will Stutely was caught, and the Sheriff ordered him to be hanged in Nottingham.
When the news reached Sherwood, Robin's company grew silent.
They could laugh at danger around a fire, but a rope around a friend's neck was another matter.
Robin led them toward the town, with their weapons ready and their bright clothes hidden.
At the place of execution, Will stood bound among guards and searched the crowd with his eyes.
He asked for a weapon so that he might die fighting instead of waiting like a tied animal.
The Sheriff had no wish to make the business difficult.
Then Little John pushed through, pretending to be only a man who wanted a last word with the prisoner.
He reached Will, and suddenly the prisoner's hands were free.
A shout went up, Robin's men rushed forward, and the neat line of guards became a struggling crowd.
The company forced a passage and carried Will out of the town.
Only when the walls were behind them did Robin count his men and let himself breathe freely.
That evening, Will ate among his friends with a new understanding of what a company meant.

The Sheriff's Fine Herd

Robin bought a butcher's meat and cart and drove to Nottingham with the cheerful air of a man going to market.
His prices were astonishing, especially when a poor woman came to buy.
The other butchers thought that someone who sold so cheaply must own more cattle than he knew what to do with.
The Sheriff heard of this foolish young merchant and invited him to dinner.
He was always interested in buying valuable things from people who did not understand their value.
Robin spoke of a great herd in the forest, and the Sheriff took money and rode out to inspect it.
Among the trees, Robin pointed to deer moving through the shade.
“There are my animals,” he said.
The Sheriff understood rather too late what kind of market he had entered.
Robin's horn called the company, and the guest found himself invited to another dinner.
The food was excellent, the welcome merry, and the bill much higher than he would have chosen.
Robin sent him home alive, lighter in the purse and heavier in his hatred.
Little John also found ways to make the Sheriff's house useful to Sherwood.
After showing his strength and skill at a fair, he was taken into the Sheriff's service.
The Sheriff admired a good servant without realizing how poorly he understood the word good.
One morning John wanted breakfast, and the household's keeper of food refused to provide it.
John was not a man who thought hunger should wait politely for permission.
He found food for himself and soon met the cook, whose duty included protecting it.
The quarrel turned into a fight, and the cook proved too strong to dismiss with one easy blow.
John admired this, for he liked a companion who could make him earn his victories.
They decided that sharing a meal was a better use of their strength than smashing each other beside it.
John described forest life, and the cook agreed to leave with him.
They took valuable things from the household and carried them into Sherwood, which made their departure difficult to mistake for an ordinary holiday.
When the Sheriff was drawn into the forest again, he found a feast prepared with things he recognized rather too well.
The faces serving him were equally familiar, and his anger gave the company a fresh reason to enjoy dinner.
It was not wise to make so powerful an enemy continually angrier, but the company did not always choose wisdom when a joke was available.

Gold for the Best Arrow

The Sheriff arranged a shooting match with a golden arrow as its prize.
Surely Robin's pride would bring him into the open, where the guards could close around him.
Robin heard the plan and came anyway, dressed so poorly that no one looked twice at him.
His companions hid their fine forest clothes beneath other disguises.
The arrows struck the target, the crowd shouted, and the Sheriff watched every promising archer.
At last the shabby stranger shot best and took the golden arrow.
The Sheriff handed over the prize to the very man for whom he had laid the trap.
Robin returned safely to Sherwood, where the story improved each time it was told.
But laughter could not make the Sheriff's anger harmless, and every visit to town remained a risk.

Four Hundred Pounds

One day a knight rode through the forest with his head bent and his clothes worn thin.
Robin invited him to eat, then asked what money he carried.
The answer was so little that the company could hardly believe it.
Sir Richard had borrowed four hundred pounds to save his son from trouble, and his land stood as security for the debt.
If he failed to pay on the appointed day, the abbey that had lent the money would take his home.
Robin listened, and the feast lost its usual noisy lightness.
He lent the knight the whole amount and sent Little John to serve him on the journey.
Fine clothes and a horse followed, for Robin disliked sending a gentleman away looking like a beggar.
At the abbey, Sir Richard first asked for more time, and the men expecting his land refused.
Then he laid down the money, and their disappointment was almost worth seeing.
Later he returned to Sherwood to repay Robin, bringing fine bows and arrows for the company.
Robin had helped a stranger and gained a friend whose gratitude was more solid than a promise.

The Song That Stopped

Allan a Dale passed through the forest singing, but when Robin saw him again, the song had disappeared.
The woman he loved was to be married to an old, wealthy knight, though she wished to marry Allan.
Robin could not lend him enough money to change the arrangement, but he could lend him a whole forest full of trouble.
First he needed a friar who would help, and found one by the water with a strong body and a stronger temper.
Robin made the friar carry him across, and the friar made Robin carry him back.
Before their quarrel ended, they had both tasted the river and discovered that neither was easily frightened.
That was the beginning of their agreement.
At the church, Robin appeared as a musician while his men waited close by.
When the bride arrived, he interrupted the wedding and asked whom she truly wished to marry.
She chose Allan, and Robin's friends made sure that no one could quietly remove her choice.
The friar married them, and the forest received a singer whose voice had found its way back.
Robin never imagined that a willing bride could make the Bishop of Hereford willing too.
The bishop objected strongly, and his rich clothing made his indignation look especially important.
The old knight had expected a wedding arranged around wealth, not one interrupted by men from the woods.
But Allan's beloved was standing there herself, and Robin would not allow everyone to talk as though she were absent.
Allan had little property to place beside the knight's, but he had a promise that the girl wished to accept.
The friar's task was to join those two promises, and Robin's company supplied the rather unusual protection.
Afterward, the newly married pair went to Sherwood, where music was as welcome as skill with a bow.
Allan sang at the feasts, and the company found that a familiar adventure sounded better when someone gave it a tune.
Yet the men whom Robin had embarrassed did not forget him when the singing stopped.
The forest could hide an outlaw from a search, but could not hide his name from a king.

A King Under a Monk's Hood

King Richard came to Sherwood dressed as a monk, curious to see this famous company for himself.
Robin stopped him, took money from the travelers, and offered a forest meal in return.
The king watched the men obey Robin and wondered why an outlaw could command so much loyalty.
After the meal came shooting, with a blow as the penalty for a poor arrow.
Robin missed, and the disguised king gave him a blow that taught him royal hands could be heavy.
When Richard revealed himself, the company fell to its knees, and the forest suddenly felt rather small.
Robin asked forgiveness, and the king offered it along with a place in his service.
Robin left with several friends, promising to visit the greenwood again.
At court he rose in favor, but promises made among leaves do not always fit the business of kings.
Years passed before he returned, and the paths remembered him better than the busy world did.

Where the Last Arrow Fell

When Robin sounded his horn again in Sherwood, Little John heard it and ran toward the old meeting place.
Their reunion held laughter and tears, because friendship had survived while so much else had changed.
Robin returned to forest life, but age and sickness came to him as they come to men who have never drawn a bow.
He went to Kirklees to have blood taken as a treatment, and the woman caring for him let too much flow.
Weak and locked in a room, he understood that he would not leave it walking.
He found enough strength to blow his horn, and Little John came, breaking in to reach him.
John wanted revenge, but Robin refused to let the house be burned for his sake.
Instead he asked for his bow and an open window.
His fingers drew the string one final time, and the arrow flew toward the place where he wished to lie.
Little John buried him there, beneath the trees that had sheltered their best years.
The company was scattered, and the old feasts could not be called back.
Yet a road still led into Sherwood, and anyone remembering Robin might hear laughter beneath its leaves.
